Ambition 2045 Overview
1. What is “Ambition 2045”?
“Ambition 2045” refers to USLT’s process of developing a shared vision for US Program work through the
foundation’s close in 2045. It is both a strategy and an implementation effort – the foundation on which USP
will align and operate over the next 20 years. Our purpose is to set bold, measurable goals that ensure our
work remains mission-aligned, impactful, achievable, and adaptive to new opportunities.
2. Why are we doing this now?
Bill’s announcement of the close of the foundation in 2045 gives us a clear 20-year horizon. This is a pivotal
moment to define US Program’s legacy and sharpen our priorities on the most critical levers for impact in
education based on our comparative advantage.
3. What is our 2045 Goal and Theory of Action?
USLT is in process of defining the 2045 goal, which will be grounded in every learner having what they need to
achieve upward mobility. We are nearing the end of this development process and will share the completed
goal architecture by the end of Feb 2026, if not sooner. As we finalize the 2045 goal, USLT will continue to
explore how to integrate work across program and functional strategy teams through a unified theory of action.
Starting with 2030, we are setting measurable targets in five-year increments (what we are calling Horizons) to
reach the 2045 goal.
4. Is our 2045 ambition going to be universal?
Yes. USP is applying a targeted universalist frame to this work. This means that we will set universal goals for
all that will be pursued through strategies that will be targeted at specific barriers and opportunities. By
investing in solutions and approaches that meet the greatest needs, we invest in solutions and approaches
that benefit all learners.
5. Will we still have focus populations?
Yes. Our approach remains grounded in targeted universalism: we set universal goals for all learners, then
identify which groups are furthest from those goals and take targeted action to remove the specific barriers
they face. We will continue to build expertise in working alongside focus populations to remove barriers, and
this will continue to shape how we design and implement strategies.

6. Is this a division-wide strategy refresh or a repackaging of what we already do?
Ambition 2045 isn’t a repackaging of our existing work — it’s a chance to step back and look across the whole
U.S. Programs portfolio to ask: Are we setting ourselves up to have the biggest possible impact for learners by
2045? We’ll absolutely build on what’s already working, but this process is meant to stretch our thinking about
long-term outcomes, where the field is heading, and how our strategies may need to evolve over time.
7. Will we still focus on technology-enabled solutions? How much on AI?
Technology-enabled solutions remain a central focus of our work and continue to be a comparative advantage
in our US Program work. In fact, our 2030 priorities are explicitly grounded in advancing AI-enabled learning
and navigation. We will be focused on demonstrating efficacy and scalability by 2030 and build toward a 2045
vision where all learners are fully supported through human-centered, AI-enabled, personalized experiences.
At the same time, we’ll stay flexible: our strategy emphasizes outcomes rather than fixed solutions or delivery
models, so we can adapt as technologies evolve and ensure solutions serve as a public good.
8. With the wind-down of the EMO team, do we still consider economic mobility and opportunity as relevant
to USP’s work?
Yes, economic mobility and opportunity remain core to USP’s vision and goals. While our primary lever is U.S.
education, the ultimate aim of that work is to enable more people to thrive in work and life. The wind-down of
the EMO team does not signal a shift away from this goal. Instead, we are sharpening our focus on how
education systems can better prepare learners to succeed as they navigate an evolving economy.
9. NEW 11/19 It seems we don't have much fixed for 2045. Is that intentional?
For this work, we have set bold, measurable goals for 2030. Then we will re-assess and consider strategic
direction for the next horizon for 2035, once we have new information. New technologies and AI-enabled
innovations are changing the education sector rapidly. We need to build in some degree of flexibility to be
responsive to the unknown. Although our vision remains the same, USP will need to be ready to pivot when we
encounter breakthroughs, shocks, or other disruptions to our ecosystem and partners.
10.What do we mean by “horizons”?
The foundation is thinking about the next 20 years in horizon segments (e.g., 5 years per horizon) to demarcate
periods of time, which will allow us to align language and timing when setting goals. In our current planning,
Horizon 1 includes the time from now until 2030, Horizon 2 would go to 2035, and so on.
Horizon 1: 2030
11.NEW 11/19 What are the draft 2030 priorities?
USLT has identified four AI-enabled solution priorities that will be supported by Delivery and Innovation, and
four cross-cutting priorities to support and enhance those solutions. These are all still in development and will
evolve as our thinking and innovations occur.
1. Full stack core instruction and tutoring
2. AI-enabled gateway math courses
3. AI-enabled personalized advising
4. AI-enabled credit mobility platforms
5. Delivery – continuing to be defined

US Program | Ambition 2045 CONFIDENTIAL
November 7, 2025
An unprecedented opportunity
Earlier this year, the Gates Foundation announced that it will double its spending over the next 20 years to accelerate progress against three
main goals: ending preventable deaths for mothers and children, eradicating diseases like polio and Guinea worm, and lifting millions out of
poverty and onto a path to prosperity.
In the United States, the foundation will continue to focus its efforts on improving U.S. education as the main lever for lifting millions out of
poverty. This work will be rooted in an ambitious vision:
Education will be a true engine of upward mobility, meeting learners with human-centered, AI-enabled support at the key points in
their learning journey. By 2045, [TBD%] more Americans will attain valued credentials that recognize their skills and provide
measurable economic returns.
To achieve this 2045 vision, USP is setting critical path priorities with related targets for 2030 that will guide its work and assess progress on
whether we are on track to achieve the 2045 vision. These priorities and targets will focus on innovation that helps learners succeed at five
pivotal moments in their educational journeys:
• success in 9th grade algebra
• quality dual enrollment in high school
• transition from high school to enroll in a postsecondary to pathway
• completing the first courses required for postsecondary credentials and remaining in college
• completing a credential/degree that includes all the appropriate credit for the courses students take towards degrees
Evidence shows that the more students who complete these milestones, the more students can earn a credential that brings economic
mobility to them, their families, and communities. What is more exciting, AI creates an unprecedented moment and potential to have
solutions that can measurably improve outcomes in these pivotal moments at a scale that leaves no one behind.
Bold & achievable targets for 2030
As part of the USP’s work to achieve this 2045 vision, we are taking this moment to ensure we have the clarity, alignment, and focus on where
our role and our dollars matter most against driving our goals. We are anchoring on eight critical path priorities that will be our explicit focus
over the next five years. Of these eight priorities, we have completed goals and target setting for four – the key solution priorities. These
goals focus on impact, scale, and affordability, and they translate our strategies into the clarity, alignment, and focus we will need to deliver
measurable progress for learners and educators across the country. The four solutions priorities are:
1. AI-Enabled Full Stack Core Instruction and Tutoring to Drive Success in 9th Grade Algebra
WHY: Students who fall one to three grade levels behind in math often need five to seven years to catch up. The best human tutors now
can accelerate learning by up to 1.6 years in one year. By 2030, we believe that it is possible for AI-powered instructional tools (HQIM)
and tutoring platforms to help accelerate student learning at levels that the best human tutors can achieve.
OUR GOAL: AI-enabled high-quality instructional materials (HQIM) and tutor platforms demonstrate accelerated learning growth—
gaining 1.6 grade levels of learning per year—across 25–40 demonstration sites, reaching up to 30,000 students. We will also continue
scaling HQIM in our focus states that will reach up to 7.4M elementary students (67%) and 4.4M middle school students (74%) at a cost
($60-$80) per student that is affordable for districts.
2. AI-Enabled Gateway Math Courses to Drive Success in College Math
WHY: Introductory or prerequisite (“gateway”) math courses are a common barrier to college completion, with national baseline
success rates averaging 70–77%. At focus institutions, baseline success rates are even lower—67–72%—highlighting gaps across
student groups. When students take and pass these courses, including through dual enrollment in high school, they expand their
postsecondary and career opportunities. By 2030, AI enabled courseware could increase pass rates, reduce gaps, and lower rates of
withdrawal while increasing movement of under-represented students in highly compensated STEM fields.
OUR GOAL: Raise pass rates by 10 percentage points (to around 80%) and cut outcome gaps in half, helping more students successfully
move toward a degree or credential of value. This effort will reach 355,000 students across 450 institutions, leading to 38,000–52,000
additional course completions through high-quality, AI-enabled courseware that improves learning outcomes for all students, at savings
up to 20% over comparable systems.
3. AI-Enabled Personalized Advising to Drive Higher Rates of Enrolling in and Completing Credentials
WHY: Navigating the pathway from high school to college derails millions of students—with only 62% of U.S. students directly enrolling
in a postsecondary program. In addition, PS completion rates are roughly 63% and only 35% for the institutions that we focus on to reach
our focus population. By 2030, functions embedded in AI large language models (LLMs) will reach up to 8.6 million high school students
and AI-enabled foundation-supported tools embedded in PS intuitions will be used by 300,000 college students. Together, these
technologies will support students through application, enrollment, and college persistence and completion
OUR GOAL: For systems using our solutions: we will raise immediate PS enrollment for systems using our solutions to by 2-5 percentage
points (between 64% and 69%) – and increase enrollment for first generation students by up to 5 percentage points. We will increase first
-year postsecondary retention by 4 to 7 percentage points (best in class today would increase by 3-4 percentage points for the same
time) and increase credential completions by 5 to 7 percentage points from the baseline of the institution

US Program: Ambition 2030/2045
Updated June 2025 – CONFIDENTIAL – For internal use only
Guiding principles for goal setting: Our goals will be…
1. Mission-aligned and impact-driven
Goals should directly support the foundation’s 2045 ambition to break the cycle of poverty and expand
paths to prosperity, and the US Program vision that all youth have what they need to thrive. They should
be designed to drive meaningful progress—not just signal intent—and inform real-world decisions,
resource allocation, and program design.
2. Aspirational, achievable, and measurable
Goals must be clear and bold enough to inspire and stretch our work, while remaining grounded in
what’s possible given our time horizon, capabilities, and comparative advantage. Goals should include
clear outcomes, success indicators, and time frames to support accountability and learning across
every team. 2030 milestones will track progress and ensure alignment.
3. Equity-centered with a Targeted Universalism approach
We pursue universal ambitions through strategies that are tailored to the specific needs of
communities and places – particularly those most impacted by systemic barriers. Where appropriate,
we should set goals for working in place in ways that will contribute to broader national or systemic
progress.
4. Connected to existing priorities and aligned with work in the field
Goals should build on and amplify existing foundation and field momentum, align with partner
priorities, and leverage the influence of our current investments, relationships, and capabilities to
maximize impact.
5. Resonant, communicable, and enduring
Goals should be clear, compelling, and emotionally resonant to a wide range of stakeholders – sta(cid:431),
grantees, partners, and communities alike. They must be easily communicated, bridge diverse
perspectives, and stand the test of time, including shifts in leadership, political context, and
technological change (e.g., centering humanized AI for good).
6. Grounded in evidence and oriented to the future
Goals should be informed by the best available research on what drives e(cid:431)ective teaching, learning,
economic mobility, and human thriving – ensuring we “walk into the future backward," anchored in
evidence while anticipating emerging needs and innovations such as AI.
Guiding principles for goal setting: As we do this work, we will…
 Hold ourselves accountable to acting as one division leadership team, prioritizing our shared mission
over individual interests and embodying alignment, trust, and shared responsibility;
 Draw on learnings from our previous work and that of foundation colleagues;
 Track our impact by measuring progress towards interim goals, accounting for the time it takes to
change systems and drive outcomes in education;
 Consider ways that working in place can improve delivery models and increase the likelihood of
success at scale;
 Rely on directors to ask for team input when we need to fill in gaps in knowledge or information;
 Ask for input from key external partners, experts and stakeholders when clarity is needed and when we
haven’t previously done so; given the short timeline, we will rely on directors to bring previous
extensive community stakeholder input into this goal-setting work;
 Show care for current sta(cid:431) and partners who will be a(cid:431)ected as we make changes and intentionally
plan for transitions;
 Be clear, wherever possible, about what we are not doing.
FAQs
1. What is “walking into the future backwards?”
a. The concept of “walking into the future backwards” comes from some Indigenous Pacific and
Māori worldviews, where the future is seen as behind us—unseen and unknowable—while the
past is in front of us, visible and known. This metaphor suggests we move forward through time
while guided by our history, rather than by trying to predict an uncertain future.
2. Will all USP teams be reflected in the goal?
a. All of our work in USP contributes to our long-term success. Our goal will be largely linked
to a set of critical path priorities—areas where we believe we have the greatest opportunity
to create lasting impact and where our capabilities and partnerships position us to deliver
impact. Not every team may be directly named or centered in the articulation of the goal or
the critical path priorities..
3. Will new strategies be considered?
a. Our priorities and strategies are largely set for the foreseeable future. At the same time, we will
continue to determine the degree our strategies and priorities are positioned for the impact we
expect to see and make any adjustments along the way that we believe are needed to meet our
2045 ambition.
4. Will interim goals be set? (i.e. 2030)
a. Yes; in fact, we are most focused on our 2030 goals at this stage. We expect to pull up in 2030
and reassess and recalibrate our initial 2045 goal as needed based on what we learn and
accomplish over the next 5 years.
b. In addition, our 2030 and 2045 goals will include milestones underneath them.
5. How will we ensure community voice and co-creation for these goals on a short timeline?
a. As mentioned in our guiding principles above, we will primarily rely on the deep community
voice and engagement our teams have done as part of developing strategies. However, if/when
it is clear that we are missing knowledge or perspective during our goal setting, we will find the
right partner or expert to provide the missing context.

Key Findings
1. Low familiarity with the Gates Foundation’s education work. Awareness of the Foundation’s role in U.S. education

replacing them.
6. Trust built through action. Concerns about teacher displacement, personalization limits, critical thinking, and tech
fatigue run deep. Messaging alone will not resolve these issues; lasting trust will depend on transparency, credible
evidence, and tangible results over time.

Doubling Down: Sharpening Our Focus on Education in the U.S. Program
By Allan Golston, President, U.S. Program, Gates Foundation.
March 31, 2026, to be posted here.

Over the past several months, I've spent a lot of time reflecting on what this moment
means for our work — and why it feels di(cid:431)erent.
Two things are true at once. The Gates Foundation now has a defined end date: we will
accelerate our spending and impact and close by 2045. That gives our work a new sense of
urgency and focus. Every dollar, every partnership, every bet we make needs to count.
At the same time, we are living through a period of genuine technological transformation. AI
is changing what's possible in education in ways that weren't imaginable even a few years
ago.
Those two realities together — a finite window and an expanding set of tools — are what's
shaping how I'm thinking about our U.S. Program work.
This is an extraordinary opportunity – and a unique challenge. We have a chance to be
bolder, more focused, and to ensure that every dollar spent moves us meaningfully closer
to better outcomes for students.
AI, in particular, presents a significant opportunity in education—not as a replacement for
teachers, but as a powerful tool to help teachers teach and help students learn, and
provide experiences and supports that enable students to pursue their goals.
The question is not whether innovation will shape education. It already is. The question is
how we harness it intentionally in service of learning and in support of teachers, and how
we make progress and opportunity possible for every student, not a select few.
I’m a firm believer that education changes lives because it changed mine. I know firsthand
how much a student’s opportunities can depend on where they grow up.
I’ve seen this most clearly when visiting schools just a few miles apart—one where
students have access to supportive teachers, advanced coursework, counselors who know
them by name, and clear pathways to college and careers, and another where equally
talented students are doing everything right but still face crowded classrooms, limited

At the Gates Foundation, our commitment to education is not new. For more than two

more students — especially those facing the greatest barriers — stay on track and earn

Being clear about the future we’re working toward
Over the next 20 years, we believe education must become a more reliable engine of
opportunity — with systems that meet learners where they are, support them at key
transition points, and recognize skills in ways that expand economic mobility.
New technologies, including advances in artificial intelligence (AI), have the potential to
support that vision—but only if they are developed and used responsibly. AI has presented
an opportunity to support learning in ways that were simply not possible before, as well as
the chance to accelerate innovation at the same pace we need students to catch up and
thrive.
However, AI is not a solution on its own, and it is not neutral by default. Its use in education
must be grounded in evidence, guided by educators, and designed with student safety,
privacy, and well-being at the center.

The Opportunity
Bill’s announcement last year was an unprecedented opportunity with call to action. He asked the
foundation to sharpen our focus, accelerate our giving, and make as much progress as possible towards
three goals.
Our work in education is critical and will be through the close of the foundation. We share Bill’s
conviction that the path to prosperity—for individuals, families, and communities in our country—must
include a quality education.
Ambition 2045 was developed with three principles that compel us to think di(cid:431)erently and act more
boldly.
1. Every student has the capacity to learn, grow, and succeed at high levels. When systems fail
to deliver on that promise, it is a design challenge, not a learner deficit. Achieving equitable
progress requires a universal commitment to high outcomes for all learners, paired with targeted
strategies that address the distinct barriers that some face. This is why our work in USP must move
beyond incremental change toward a more intentional alignment of focus, resources, and
systems.
2. We are at a once-in-a-generation inflection point to fully leverage what makes us uniquely
e(cid:431)ective. Greater focus and synergy on our comparative advantages – building learning systems
that accelerate progress, navigation systems that sustain momentum, and delivery mechanisms
that enable equitable scale – o(cid:431)ers a greater chance to find paths for impact that endures. We
must focus these strengths with greater clarity, urgency, and purpose.
3. Advances in AI – when paired with learners and educators – create a new horizon for what is
possible. In addition to our partners and stakeholders, we now have a rare opportunity to engage
a common set of actors across our work – including AI hyperscalers and platform leaders – with
similar levers to influence how these technologies, solutions, and supports evolve. This gives us
greater possibilities to unlock innovation that is more personalized, more accessible, and more
a(cid:431)ordable, and at a scale and speed that was previously unimaginable.

di(cid:431)erent life trajectory. Over the past seven months, USLT discussed these principles while rigorously
examining our theory of action, strategies, priorities, and the ways we are organized to deliver the impact
we seek. A division-wide strategy became an imperative, driven by the foundation’s steady-state
budget and 20-year time horizon, the rapidly changing external environment, where the pace of change in
AI and education is accelerating and our work increasingly intersects with the same partners or

collaboration and accountability.

Ambition 2045 Overview
1. What is “Ambition 2045”?
“Ambition 2045” refers to our shared vision for US Program work as the foundation accelerates giving and
impact through its eventual closure in 2045. Ambition 2045 is both the strategy and implementation effort
against which USP will align and operate over the next 20 years.
2. UPDATED 3/13 What is our 2045 Goal and Theory of Action?
By 2045, our vision is that education is a true engine of upward mobility. Our goal is to double the growth rate
of credential attainment for Americans ages 18–34. As a result, 10M more learners—and a focus on those who
face the greatest obstacles—will attain credentials that recognize their skills and knowledge and deliver
sustained economic value. To learn more about how we arrived at this goal, and to see our Theory of Action
(slide 11), please see this compendium.
3. Are we applying a targeted universalist frame to this work? Will we still have focus populations?
Yes. Our approach remains grounded in targeted universalism: we set universal goals for all learners, then
identify which groups are farthest from those goals, taking responsive, targeted action to remove the barriers
they face. For example, in PS courseware, our 2030 target includes a 10-percentage point increase in course
success rates and a 50% reduction in gaps for focus learners. We will continue to build expertise in working
alongside focus populations, and this will continue to shape how we design and implement strategies.
4. What do we mean by “horizons”?
The foundation is thinking about the next 20 years in 5-year segments that we are calling “horizons.”
Organizing ourselves in this way promotes periodic reflection, goal setting, and sensemaking in a rapidly
changing field. Horizon 1 includes the time from now until 2030, Horizon 2 would go to 2035, and so on.

6. How did USLT decide on the first four solution priorities?
By 2045, our aim is that substantially more Americans attain credentials of value that lead to economic
opportunity. As an evidence-based organization, we started with the data: We know that when learners hit key
milestones in their E-W journey, they are more likely to earn a high-value credential. Ambition 2045 is
anchored on these key moments:
 Success in 9th Grade Algebra: Developing and delivering AI-enabled, high-quality instructional materials
and integrated tutoring to accelerate math learning and close foundational gaps.
 Transition from High School to Postsecondary Pathways: Using AI-enabled personalized advising tools to
increase FAFSA completion, immediate college enrollment, and first-year persistence.
 Completion of Gateway Postsecondary Math Courses: Redesigning entry-level college math courses
through AI-powered courseware to increase success rates, cut equity gaps, and strengthen persistence
toward a credential of value.
 Credit Transfer and Learning Mobility: Building AI-driven tools and data systems that enable learners to
understand and apply credits across institutions, as well as represent the full range of skills and
experiences they’ve earned through education and employment.
Grounded in an understanding of learner needs, we also reviewed advances in AI, evolving data privacy
landscapes, and emerging policy developments; input from USP colleagues gathered through the May 2025
moonshot goal submissions; and the foundation’s comparative advantage, including our expertise, talent,
knowledge, and relationships in the areas above.
The resulting four solution priorities build on USP’s existing strengths and are designed to meet learners at
critical points in their journeys. Together, our solution priorities aim to ensure learners can equitably build and
sustain momentum across their educational journey.
7. How did USLT decide on the three additional priorities?
As USLT discussed how best to meet our goals, we landed on three central tenets that build upon our solution
specific work to further unlock opportunities to make education a true engine for economic mobility. First,
rapid advances in AI capabilities weren’t necessarily serving the use cases and populations that our strategy
will prioritize, and we are uniquely situated to ensure that the learners we serve benefit from this innovation.
Second, the K–12 and Postsecondary systems that serve learners need to be able to drive improvement
efforts, and we believe there is work to be done to drive demand and capacity on this front. Third, we think
there are breakthroughs within and adjacent to the strategy that would truly unlock opportunity for learners.
