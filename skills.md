# 1. K-12 EDUCATION STRATEGY

## 1.1 Strategic Intent

The K-12 strategy within USP aims to dramatically improve learning outcomes for students from low-income backgrounds and students of color — the populations most underserved by American public education. The foundational premise is that the existing system consistently fails to deliver equitable academic opportunity, and that strategic philanthropic investment can shift systemic conditions (instructional materials, data infrastructure, human capital pipelines, policy environment) to enable better teaching and learning at scale.

The strategic intent is not to operate schools directly, but to influence the ecosystem: funding curriculum development, supporting adoption of high-quality instructional materials (HQIM), building evidence bases, shaping policy, and increasingly, enabling AI-powered tutoring and learning tools. The overarching goal is to ensure that all students — regardless of race or income — have access to excellent teaching and rigorous, culturally relevant instruction.

## 1.2 Theory of Change

The K-12 theory of change has evolved substantially over 2019-2026, but its persistent structure is:

**If** high-quality instructional materials (HQIM) are placed in teachers' hands, teachers receive adequate professional development and support, and district and state policy enables adoption and fidelity of use, **then** students will experience improved instructional quality, which leads to improved academic outcomes, which leads to reduced achievement gaps and improved life trajectories for students from low-income backgrounds.

The 2025 iteration adds an AI layer: **If** AI tutoring tools are developed with rigorous evidence standards, are integrated with HQIM, and are accessible to underserved students, **then** instructional support can be personalized and extended beyond the classroom, accelerating learning gains.

The theory of change has consistently held that:
- **Materials matter more than general teacher quality programs.** Weak curricula cannot be overcome by talented teachers alone.
- **Algebra 1 is a gateway course.** Success in Algebra 1 predicts postsecondary readiness more than almost any other single milestone. Failure in Algebra 1 often forecloses postsecondary opportunity.
- **Districts are the unit of change.** Individual school interventions don't scale; district-level adoption decisions and policy choices are where leverage lies.
- **States set the enabling conditions.** State curriculum adoption policies, accountability frameworks, and professional development requirements shape what districts can do.

## 1.3 Core Concepts & Frameworks

**High-Quality Instructional Materials (HQIM):** Curriculum materials — textbooks, unit plans, lesson sequences, assessments — that are rated highly by independent review bodies (EdReports, Louisiana Believes, SBAC alignment reviews). HQIM is the foundation of the post-2019 K-12 strategy. The prior approach to "teacher effectiveness" (value-added measures, evaluation systems) was substantially deprioritized in favor of ensuring quality materials are in classrooms.

**EdReports:** An independent nonprofit that reviews K-12 curriculum materials against alignment to college and career-ready standards. The Gates Foundation has been a significant funder of EdReports and uses its ratings to define what counts as HQIM. Materials with "Meets Expectations" ratings across all three gateways (Focus & Coherence, Rigor & Mathematical Practices/ELA alignment, Usability) are considered HQIM.

**Instructional Materials + Professional Development (IM+PD):** The paired strategy of ensuring both quality materials AND quality professional development are co-deployed. The research base shows that HQIM alone, without support for teachers to implement it with fidelity, produces limited gains.

**Algebra 1 as a Gateway:** An explicit strategic focus on Algebra 1 as the single most important course in the K-12 pipeline. Students who fail or are tracked out of Algebra 1 by 9th grade face dramatically reduced chances of college preparation. The K-12 strategy targets middle school math (grades 6-8) and Algebra 1 specifically as the highest-leverage intervention points.

**Coherence and Rigor:** The two non-negotiable properties of quality math curriculum per the Common Core State Standards framework. Coherence refers to the logical, building-block sequencing of mathematical ideas across grades. Rigor refers to the balanced treatment of procedural fluency, conceptual understanding, and application.

**Common Core State Standards (CCSS):** The set of academic standards in ELA and Mathematics adopted by 41+ states, developed with Gates Foundation support in 2009-2010. Common Core alignment is a prerequisite for HQIM designation. The political controversy over Common Core (especially post-2012) created headwinds for the broader K-12 strategy and is a persistent tension.

**AI Tutoring:** The 2025-era strategic bet that AI-powered tutoring tools (conversational, adaptive, integrated with HQIM) can provide individualized instructional support at scale, particularly for students who lack access to high-quality human tutors. This is the newest layer of the K-12 theory of change.

**Structured Literacy / Science of Reading:** An adjacent (but increasingly central) focus area on evidence-based reading instruction. The "Science of Reading" movement — emphasizing phonics, phonemic awareness, and explicit decoding instruction — has gained significant policy traction 2022-2025, and the Gates Foundation's K-12 strategy has incorporated support for literacy HQIM.

## 1.4 Sub-strategies

**Math HQIM Adoption:** Directly support the selection and adoption of high-quality math curricula in districts and states. This includes funding the development of HQIM materials (e.g., Illustrative Mathematics, Open Up Resources), supporting state curriculum review processes, and funding districts' implementation of HQIM.

**ELA HQIM Adoption:** Parallel to math, but more politically contested given debates about literacy instruction approaches. Support evidence-based reading programs, structured literacy approaches, and quality ELA curriculum development.

**Professional Development & Coaching:** Fund professional development organizations and platforms that build teacher and coach capacity to implement HQIM with fidelity. Key grantees include The New Teacher Project (TNTP), Learning Forward, and curriculum-specific PD providers.

**Evidence Building:** Fund research and evaluation studies that build the evidence base for HQIM's impact on student outcomes. Support RAND's work on curriculum use and impact; fund IES-aligned RCTs and quasi-experimental studies.

**AI Tools Integration:** Invest in development of AI tutoring tools that are aligned with HQIM, rigorously evaluated, and designed for equitable access. Explore partnerships with AI developers (Khan Academy's Khanmigo, Carnegie Learning, etc.) and fund research on AI tutoring efficacy.

**State Policy:** Advocate for state-level policies that encourage HQIM adoption — open textbook requirements, curriculum transparency laws, and professional development standards tied to HQIM implementation.

## 1.5 Key Vocabulary

- **HQIM:** High-Quality Instructional Materials — curriculum rated as meeting standards by independent reviewers
- **EdReports:** Independent curriculum review nonprofit; primary arbiter of HQIM status
- **Algebra 1 gateway:** The strategic premise that Algebra 1 success is the single most predictive K-12 milestone
- **IM (Illustrative Mathematics):** A widely adopted HQIM math curriculum developed with philanthropic support
- **OUR (Open Up Resources):** Publisher of open-source HQIM; partner with Illustrative Mathematics
- **Coherence:** Logical, grade-to-grade building of mathematical ideas; a CCSS principle
- **Rigor:** Balance of procedural fluency, conceptual understanding, and application; a CCSS principle
- **Tier 1 curriculum:** Highest-quality designation under EdReports review framework
- **Science of Reading:** Evidence-based approach to literacy instruction emphasizing phonics and explicit decoding
- **PD (Professional Development):** Teacher training and coaching support for curriculum implementation
- **Value-added measure (VAM):** Statistical approach to estimating teacher effectiveness from student test score growth; controversial and now deprioritized
- **IES (Institute of Education Sciences):** Federal research arm; key partner for evidence standards
- **SBAC (Smarter Balanced Assessment Consortium):** Multi-state consortium developing Common Core-aligned assessments

## 1.6 Named Programs, Initiatives, and Grantees

- **Illustrative Mathematics (IM):** Major HQIM math curriculum; free, openly licensed; widely adopted in K-8 and high school math
- **Open Up Resources:** Publisher partnering with IM to distribute and support implementation
- **EdReports:** Funded by Gates; produces independent curriculum quality ratings
- **TNTP (The New Teacher Project):** Teacher effectiveness and PD organization; grantee for implementation support
- **RAND Corporation:** Research partner for curriculum use studies, HQIM evidence base
- **Khan Academy / Khanmigo:** AI tutoring platform; explored as a model for AI-assisted instruction
- **Carnegie Learning:** AI-integrated math curriculum company; one of few HQIM providers with an embedded adaptive learning engine
- **Learning Forward:** Professional development standards and capacity organization
- **Student Achievement Partners:** Nonprofit founded by Common Core authors (Zimba, McCallum, Berger); develops curriculum support tools and resources; frequent OOP strategy partners
- **Louisiana Believes:** Louisiana DOE's curriculum review and adoption system; often cited as a model state for HQIM implementation
- **Achieve the Core:** Student Achievement Partners' website with free HQIM resources

## 1.7 Outcome Metrics

- **NAEP (National Assessment of Educational Progress) scores:** The "Nation's Report Card"; primary external benchmark for K-12 achievement
- **Algebra 1 completion by 9th grade:** Percentage of students completing Algebra 1 by end of 9th grade; key leading indicator
- **HQIM adoption rates:** Percentage of districts/schools using EdReports-rated Tier 1 materials
- **Achievement gap metrics:** NAEP score gaps between white and Black/Hispanic students; between high- and low-income students
- **Proficiency rates:** State assessment proficiency in math and ELA; used as a proxy for college and career readiness
- **AP/IB enrollment and pass rates:** Proxy for advanced learning access among low-income students and students of color
- **Teacher PD completion:** Percentage of teachers receiving HQIM-aligned professional development
- **State curriculum transparency:** Number of states publishing curriculum adoption data publicly
- **AI tutoring usage and efficacy:** Student engagement with AI tutoring tools; learning gain measurements from efficacy studies

## 1.8 Geographic & Demographic Focus

**Primary demographic target:** Students from low-income backgrounds and students of color — specifically Black, Hispanic/Latino, and Native American students — who face the most significant barriers to rigorous instruction.

**Demographic context:** Students of color became the majority of US public school enrollment between 2000 and 2017, and were projected to reach 56% of enrollment by 2029. In 2017-18, 79% of teachers were white and 77.7% of principals were white — creating a persistent demographic mismatch in the educator pipeline.

**Geographic focus:** The K-12 strategy is primarily national in scope, working through states and large districts. However, Texas has been a focal state for USP Data work embedded in K-12. Louisiana has been a model state for HQIM adoption. Washington State has a distinct strategy (see WSI section). Large urban districts (Chicago, New York, Los Angeles, Houston) are often implementation partners.

## 1.9 Strategic Shifts & Inflection Points

**2013-2018: Teacher Effectiveness Era.** The foundation's K-12 strategy was heavily focused on teacher effectiveness — developing and implementing evaluation systems with value-added measures, supporting alternative certification pipelines, and investing in human capital. The Intensive Partnerships for Effective Teaching initiative ($575M, 2009-2016) was the flagship. Outcomes were disappointing; a RAND evaluation found minimal impacts on student achievement.

**2019: Pivot to HQIM.** Following the Intensive Partnerships evaluation and internal strategic reviews, the K-12 strategy pivoted to high-quality instructional materials as the primary lever. The logic: even great teachers cannot overcome weak curriculum; better to get excellent materials into every classroom. This was a major strategic reset.

**2020-2022: Consolidation and deepening.** The HQIM strategy deepened, with investment in ELA alongside math, increased focus on Algebra 1 as a gateway, and growing attention to the Science of Reading movement. COVID-19 disrupted implementation but also accelerated digital adoption and created urgency around learning recovery.

**2023-2025: AI integration.** The emergence of large language models (GPT-4, Gemini, etc.) created new possibilities for AI tutoring. The K-12 strategy began integrating AI tutoring as a new layer, building on HQIM foundations. Questions about how to evaluate AI tools, who benefits, and how to ensure equitable access became central.

**Persistent tension:** The Common Core political controversy remains a complicating factor. The foundation's visible sponsorship of Common Core development generated significant political backlash; the K-12 strategy has navigated carefully around the Common Core label while continuing to support CCSS-aligned materials.

## 1.10 Tensions & Tradeoffs

- **Scale vs. depth:** HQIM adoption at national scale requires working with many states and districts, sacrificing deep implementation support. Deep PD support is expensive and hard to scale.
- **Evidence standards vs. speed:** Building a rigorous evidence base for AI tutoring takes years; the technology is moving fast and advocates argue waiting for evidence means losing the window.
- **Equity and access:** AI tutoring tools risk exacerbating inequity if students with better devices, connectivity, and tech support benefit most. Ensuring equitable access is a persistent design challenge.
- **Common Core politics:** Supporting CCSS-aligned HQIM is implicitly supporting Common Core in states where it is politically toxic. The strategy often avoids the "Common Core" label while funding materials that require CCSS alignment.
- **Teacher agency:** Strong curriculum mandates (HQIM with fidelity) can conflict with teacher professional autonomy and union positions. The field debates whether fidelity or adaptation produces better outcomes.
- **Materials quality vs. cultural relevance:** HQIM quality reviews focus on academic rigor and standards alignment but have historically given less attention to cultural responsiveness. These dimensions can be in tension.

# 2. POSTSECONDARY SUCCESS STRATEGY

## 2.1 Strategic Intent

The Postsecondary Success (PS) strategy aims to dramatically increase the number of students from low-income backgrounds who complete high-quality postsecondary credentials with economic value. The strategy is built on the recognition that postsecondary completion has become the primary gateway to economic mobility, and that the existing system systematically fails low-income students, first-generation students, and students of color at every stage: access, persistence, completion, and transition to employment.

The foundational equity framing is that a student's race or family income should not predict their likelihood of postsecondary completion or the economic value of their credential. The strategy works across multiple institutional types — four-year universities, community colleges, and increasingly, workforce-aligned programs — to improve systems, policies, and practices that affect student outcomes.

## 2.2 Theory of Change

The PS theory of change operates at three levels:

**Institutional level:** If institutions (colleges and universities) adopt data-informed practices, redesign advising and support services, eliminate unnecessary barriers (excess credits, remediation inefficiencies, scheduling constraints), and align curricula to labor market needs, then more students from low-income backgrounds will persist and complete.

**System/policy level:** If state and federal policies create the right incentives (performance-based funding, completion-focused accountability, equitable finance, simplified financial aid), then institutions will have both the mandate and the resources to improve outcomes for underserved students.

**Market/information level:** If students, families, and counselors have access to clear, reliable information about credential quality, cost, and economic value — and if the definition of "quality" encompasses equitable outcomes — then market forces and student choice will reward high-performing institutions and press low-performing ones to improve.

The theory recognizes that all three levels must move together; policy without institutional capacity, or institutional change without policy support, produces limited results.

## 2.3 Core Concepts & Frameworks

**Equitable Value:** The principle that postsecondary credentials should deliver measurable economic and social value specifically for students from low-income backgrounds and students of color. "Value" is defined across three dimensions: economic value (earnings and employment), academic quality (rigor, credential relevance), and equity (whether outcomes are equitable across race and income groups). The equitable value framework is a reframing away from simply increasing completion numbers toward ensuring those completions deliver meaningful returns.

**Institutional Transformation:** One of the four strategic pillars. The theory is that institutions can be changed from within through data use, leadership development, and practice redesign. The Student Success Work — advising reform, early alert systems, meta-majors, corequisite remediation — falls under this pillar.

**Public Goods:** The second pillar. Some high-value resources should be available to all institutions and students rather than locked in proprietary systems. Open courseware, shared data infrastructure (PDP), and shared tools for measuring student success fall under this pillar.

**National Scale Enterprises:** The third pillar. A small number of high-quality postsecondary providers can grow to serve hundreds of thousands of students at a time, delivering quality and value at scale. Western Governors University (WGU) and Southern New Hampshire University (SNHU) are exemplars. The strategy includes supporting models that can achieve national scale.

**Corequisite Remediation:** A redesign of the traditional remediation (developmental education) model in which students who test below college-level are placed into non-credit developmental courses before being allowed to take credit-bearing courses. Corequisite redesigns allow students to enroll directly in college-level gateway courses while receiving concurrent academic support. Evidence shows dramatically higher gateway course completion rates with corequisite models — a rare high-confidence finding in PS research.

**Student Success Work / Guided Pathways:** A framework for institutional reform developed by the Community College Research Center (CCRC) and the American Association of Community Colleges (AACC). The four pillars are: map pathways to student end goals, help students choose and enter a pathway, keep students on their path, and ensure students are learning. Many Gates PS investments are aligned to Guided Pathways principles.

**Meta-Majors:** Broad program groupings that help undecided students explore academic interests without losing credits when they change direction. A Guided Pathways concept.

**Momentum Metrics / Velocity Metrics:** Leading indicators of student success measured early in enrollment — credits earned in first semester, gateway course completion, enrollment in second semester. These are more actionable than degree completion rates because they are measured in real time.

**Postsecondary Data Partnership (PDP):** A national data infrastructure platform managed by the National Student Clearinghouse (NSC) that allows institutions to share and analyze student outcome data. By 2022, PDP covered approximately one-third of all postsecondary enrollment. PDP is a public goods investment in shared data infrastructure.

**Workforce Alignment:** The strategic imperative that postsecondary credentials must connect to jobs that provide family-sustaining wages. This drives investment in labor market information, program-level earnings data, and industry partnerships.

## 2.4 Sub-strategies

**Institutional Transformation:** Working with colleges and universities to redesign student success systems — advising, early alert, corequisite remediation, degree pathway mapping. Key partners include CCRC, AACC, Achieving the Dream.

**Community College Focus:** Community colleges serve the largest share of low-income and first-generation students. A significant portion of PS investment targets community college reform, including through the Guided Pathways framework.

**Public Goods Infrastructure:** Building and sustaining shared platforms — especially the Postsecondary Data Partnership (PDP) and open courseware libraries — that benefit the entire postsecondary sector.

**National Scale Models:** Supporting and scaling providers with demonstrated quality who can grow to serve hundreds of thousands of students. WGU, SNHU, and potentially new models built on AI-powered personalized learning.

**Federal and State Policy:** Advocating for policy changes that create the right incentives — performance-based funding, simplified FAFSA, first-dollar-free college models, equitable accountability frameworks, and student loan reform.

**Equitable Value Evidence Building:** Funding research and data analysis to define, measure, and communicate the concept of equitable value — including program-level outcome data by race and income.

## 2.5 Key Vocabulary

- **Equitable value:** Credentials that deliver measurable economic and social value specifically for low-income students and students of color
- **Corequisite remediation:** Concurrent support model replacing sequential developmental education
- **Guided Pathways:** Framework for institutional reform; four pillars: map, choose, stay on path, learn
- **Meta-major:** Broad academic grouping enabling exploration without credit loss
- **Momentum metrics:** Early indicators of student success (credits earned, gateway completion, persistence)
- **PDP (Postsecondary Data Partnership):** NSC-managed shared data platform covering ~1/3 of PS enrollment
- **Throughput:** Students moving through the pipeline from enrollment to completion
- **Completion rates:** Share of entering students who earn a credential within a defined period
- **Persistence rates:** Share of students who return for a second year/term
- **Labor market value / ROI:** Economic return to a postsecondary credential in terms of earnings and employment
- **First-dollar free:** Financial aid model in which all college costs are covered before other aid is applied (vs. last-dollar free)
- **Pell Grant:** Federal need-based grant; evidence shows $1,000 in Pell increases enrollment 3-5%
- **Title IV accountability:** Federal accountability requirements tied to institutional eligibility for federal financial aid
- **FAFSA (Free Application for Federal Student Aid):** Federal financial aid application; simplification is a major policy priority
- **WGU (Western Governors University):** National-scale, competency-based, online university; exemplar of a high-quality PS scale model
- **SNHU (Southern New Hampshire University):** National-scale online university; another PS scale model exemplar
- **CCRC (Community College Research Center):** Columbia University research center; produces PS research and Guided Pathways content
- **AACC (American Association of Community Colleges):** Primary sector association; partner for Guided Pathways dissemination
- **Achieving the Dream:** Network of community colleges focused on student success improvement

## 2.6 Named Programs, Initiatives, and Grantees

- **National Student Clearinghouse (NSC):** Manages PDP and provides enrollment/completion tracking data
- **Community College Research Center (CCRC):** Columbia research center; Guided Pathways research and technical assistance
- **American Association of Community Colleges (AACC):** Partner for Guided Pathways adoption across the sector
- **Achieving the Dream:** Network of ~300 community colleges focused on student success
- **Western Governors University (WGU):** Scale model PS provider; competency-based; serves over 150,000 students
- **Southern New Hampshire University (SNHU):** Scale model; College for America program serves low-income adults
- **Complete College America:** Advocacy organization promoting corequisite remediation and completion reforms
- **Student Achievement Partners / Achieve the Core:** Curriculum support (more K-12 focused but crosses into PS transitions)
- **Lumina Foundation:** Co-investor with Gates in many PS strategy areas
- **Jobs for the Future (JFF):** Workforce and pathways organization; co-investor
- **American Institutes for Research (AIR):** Research and evaluation partner
- **Third Way:** Policy think tank; equitable value framing work
- **New America:** Policy organization; financial aid and student debt research
- **Center on Budget and Policy Priorities (CBPP):** State higher education finance policy
- **Ben Miller / Center for American Progress (CAP):** Federal PS policy partnerships; April 2020 Gates-CAP event on federal policy

## 2.7 Outcome Metrics

- **Credential completion rates:** Share of entering students completing any credential within 6 years (or other timeframes)
- **Equity gaps in completion:** Completion rate gaps between low-income/first-gen/students of color and other students
- **Gateway course completion rates:** Pass rates in gateway math and English courses; key leading indicator
- **Transfer success rates:** Rates at which community college students successfully transfer and complete at four-year institutions
- **Earnings outcomes / labor market value:** Graduates' median earnings 1, 5, and 10 years after completion; program-level ROI
- **Debt-to-earnings ratios:** Student debt relative to earnings; used in federal accountability proposals
- **FAFSA completion rates:** Share of seniors completing FAFSA; indicator of financial aid access
- **Momentum metrics:** First-semester credits, gateway course pass rates, second-term enrollment
- **PDP enrollment and coverage:** Number of institutions and share of national enrollment covered by PDP

## 2.8 Geographic & Demographic Focus

**Primary demographic target:** Low-income students, first-generation college students, students of color (especially Black and Hispanic students), adult learners returning to education, and working students.

**Geographic focus:** National strategy, but with particular attention to states with large low-income student populations and strong policy leverage. Texas, California, Florida, and New York have large community college systems and are important states for PS strategy. Washington State is a focus state (see WSI). The foundation has also worked in several focal states through its state policy strategy.

**Institutional type focus:** Community colleges are the primary institutional target (they serve the most low-income students and have the most room for improvement). Four-year institutions, especially regional comprehensives serving low-income students, are secondary targets.

## 2.9 Strategic Shifts & Inflection Points

**Pre-2019: Completion agenda era.** The PS strategy was primarily focused on increasing credential completion rates — the "completion agenda." The strategy aligned closely with national campaigns (Complete College America, Lumina's Goal 2025) to increase the percentage of Americans with postsecondary credentials. Evidence of impact on equity was limited.

**2019-2020: Equitable value reframe.** A major strategic reframe: completion alone is insufficient if the credentials don't deliver economic value and don't narrow equity gaps. The "equitable value" framework replaced "completion" as the North Star. This reflected both evidence (many credentials have negative ROI, especially for students of color) and equity-driven critique that the completion agenda masked persistent gaps.

**2020: COVID-19 disruption.** Enrollment declines — especially at community colleges — disrupted the strategy. Community college enrollment fell dramatically in 2020-2021, with disproportionate impact on students of color and low-income students. The strategy pivoted in part to enrollment recovery and student support.

**2021-2023: Workforce alignment deepening.** Growing emphasis on labor market alignment, earnings outcomes, and the connection between PS credentials and good jobs. The "good jobs" framing and focus on stackable credentials and apprenticeships grew in this period.

**2024-2025: AI and new models.** AI-powered personalized learning and AI-assisted advising are emerging as new strategic levers in the PS space. National scale models are exploring AI integration. The PS strategy is beginning to grapple with how AI transforms what "quality instruction" means at scale.

## 2.10 Tensions & Tradeoffs

- **Completion vs. equitable value:** Institutions can increase completion rates by softening graduation requirements or awarding credentials of low economic value. The equitable value frame requires resisting this pressure.
- **Scale vs. quality:** National scale models (WGU, SNHU) can serve many students but face questions about whether their quality is genuinely comparable to residential programs.
- **Community colleges vs. four-year focus:** Community colleges serve the most students who need support, but change is slow and resource-constrained. Four-year institutions are more resource-rich but serve fewer low-income students.
- **Workforce alignment vs. liberal education:** The emphasis on earnings outcomes and labor market value can crowd out attention to civic education, personal development, and the non-economic purposes of postsecondary education.
- **Debt forgiveness vs. systemic reform:** The foundation's policy position opposes blanket student debt forgiveness (as regressive — higher-income graduates hold more debt) but supports targeted relief and structural reforms. This creates tension with progressive allies and advocates.
- **Federal vs. state leverage:** Federal policy has the broadest reach (Pell, Title IV), but state policy is where implementation happens. The strategy must balance federal advocacy and state policy work.

# 3. PATHWAYS STRATEGY

## 3.1 Strategic Intent

The Pathways strategy addresses the transition zone between secondary education (high school), postsecondary education, and the workforce. It aims to create coherent, high-quality pathways that connect students — especially those from low-income backgrounds — to postsecondary credentials and careers without the dead ends, misalignment, and loss of momentum that characterize the current system.

The strategic intent is specifically to build "quality pathways" — a term the strategy uses deliberately to distinguish pathways that lead to good outcomes (economic value, credential completion, career advancement) from pathways that may be officially recognized but lead nowhere. The framing is that the K-12 and PS systems are vertically misaligned, and the Pathways strategy fills the connective tissue between them.

## 3.2 Theory of Change

**If** high-quality career and technical education (CTE) programs, dual enrollment opportunities, early college high schools, and industry-aligned apprenticeships are available to students from low-income backgrounds **and** if navigation support (advising, mentoring, near-peer support) helps students access and complete these pathways **and** if state and local policy aligns incentives (credit transfer, FAFSA completion, pathway funding), **then** more students will complete postsecondary credentials with economic value and enter careers with family-sustaining wages.

The Pathways theory of change explicitly bridges the K-12 and PS theories of change, treating the 9th-14th grade span as a coherent unit of intervention.

## 3.3 Core Concepts & Frameworks

**Quality Pathways Framework:** The organizing framework for the Pathways strategy. A "quality pathway" has four properties: (1) it is academically rigorous and leads to a valuable credential; (2) it is transparent — students can see where it leads and what it costs; (3) it is equitable — it is accessible to and designed for students from low-income backgrounds and students of color; (4) it is connected — it links HS, PS, and workforce in a coherent sequence.

**Dual Enrollment:** High school students taking college courses for credit. When well-designed, dual enrollment increases postsecondary enrollment and completion, especially for first-generation students. The strategy supports expansion of high-quality, equitably accessible dual enrollment.

**Early College High School (ECHS):** Schools that allow students to earn up to two years of college credit while completing high school, typically in partnership with a community college. A high-evidence model for increasing PS completion for low-income students.

**CTE (Career and Technical Education):** Vocational and technical education programs that connect academic learning to career preparation. The Pathways strategy supports high-quality CTE that is aligned to labor market demand and integrated with PS credential pathways.

**Apprenticeship:** Earn-and-learn models combining on-the-job training with classroom instruction. The strategy supports expansion of apprenticeship, especially in sectors accessible to students without four-year degrees.

**Stackable Credentials:** Sequences of credentials that build on each other, allowing students to enter the workforce, earn credentials, and continue building qualifications over time. Important for adult learners and working students.

**Navigational Support:** Advising, mentoring, and near-peer support services that help students navigate the complexity of pathways — especially first-generation students who lack the social capital and family networks that middle-class students rely on.

**9th-14th Grade Span:** The Pathways strategy's unique framing of the transition as a continuous span from 9th grade through two years of postsecondary (or 14th grade), rather than treating HS and PS as entirely separate systems.

## 3.4 Sub-strategies

**Dual Enrollment Expansion:** Supporting state and local policies that expand access to high-quality dual enrollment, especially for low-income and first-generation students. Key barriers: course rigor, transportation, cost, counselor knowledge.

**Early College High School:** Supporting and scaling ECHS models, particularly in partnership with community colleges. Key grantees: Jobs for the Future (JFF), National Alliance of Concurrent Enrollment Partnerships (NACEP).

**CTE Quality Improvement:** Supporting the development and adoption of CTE programs that are industry-aligned, rigorous, and connected to PS pathways. Perkins Act reauthorization (2018) created new state planning requirements that the strategy leverages.

**Apprenticeship Expansion:** Supporting federal and state apprenticeship expansion in non-traditional sectors (healthcare, technology, business services) to widen access beyond traditional trades.

**Navigation and Advising:** Funding advising reform, near-peer mentoring, and FAFSA completion support at the HS-PS transition. WSI (see Section 4) is a place-based expression of this sub-strategy.

**State Policy Alignment:** Advocating for state policies that create coherent pathways — dual enrollment policy, credit transfer agreements, CTE funding, FAFSA completion requirements.

## 3.5 Key Vocabulary

- **Quality pathways:** Pathways meeting all four quality criteria (rigorous, transparent, equitable, connected)
- **Dual enrollment:** HS students taking college courses for credit; also called concurrent enrollment
- **ECHS (Early College High School):** HS model allowing students to earn two years of college credit
- **CTE (Career and Technical Education):** Vocational/technical education; now rebranded as "career pathways" in many states
- **Stackable credentials:** Sequential credential system allowing step-by-step credential accumulation
- **Apprenticeship:** Earn-and-learn models combining employment and instruction
- **Navigation/advising:** Services helping students access and persist through pathways
- **9th-14th grade span:** The continuous transition zone from 9th grade through second year of PS
- **Perkins Act:** Carl D. Perkins Career and Technical Education Act; federal CTE funding law
- **Credit transfer:** Portability of credits between institutions; a major barrier in current system
- **FAFSA completion:** Completing the federal financial aid application; a key transition milestone
- **Credential value / labor market alignment:** Whether pathways lead to credentials with economic value
- **Industry sector partnerships:** Employer-education partnerships defining pathway skill requirements

## 3.6 Named Programs, Initiatives, and Grantees

- **Jobs for the Future (JFF):** Major Pathways grantee; ECHS research and advocacy, CTE quality, workforce pathway work
- **NACEP (National Alliance of Concurrent Enrollment Partnerships):** Dual enrollment standards and research organization
- **College in High School Alliance:** Advocacy coalition for dual enrollment and ECHS expansion
- **Advance CTE:** State CTE directors association; policy partner for Perkins implementation
- **New America:** Policy research on dual enrollment equity
- **Arnold Ventures:** Co-funder on workforce pathways
- **Joyce Foundation:** Co-funder on Midwest pathways
- **American Institutes for Research (AIR):** Research and evaluation of ECHS and dual enrollment
- **WestEd:** Research and technical assistance on CTE quality
- **National Center for Education and the Economy (NCEE):** Policy and systems design for PS-workforce alignment

## 3.7 Outcome Metrics

- **Dual enrollment participation rates:** Share of HS students (especially low-income) taking college courses
- **ECHS graduation and PS completion rates:** Outcomes for ECHS graduates vs. comparison groups
- **CTE program completion rates:** Share of students completing CTE sequences (not just individual courses)
- **FAFSA completion rates:** Especially for students in pathway programs
- **Credential completion:** PS credential completion rates for students in pathway programs
- **Labor market outcomes:** Earnings and employment for pathway completers
- **Credit transfer success:** Percentage of dual enrollment credits successfully transferred and applied
- **Equity metrics:** Disaggregation of all outcomes by race, income, and first-generation status

## 3.8 Geographic & Demographic Focus

**Primary demographic target:** Low-income high school students, especially those in communities with limited access to rigorous coursework or postsecondary opportunities; first-generation college students; students of color.

**Geographic focus:** National with some place-based focus. Washington State is a primary focus for dual enrollment and FAFSA completion (through WSI). Several states with strong CTE systems (Texas, Tennessee, North Carolina) are important for the Pathways strategy.

## 3.9 Strategic Shifts & Inflection Points

**Pre-2019: Completion and pipeline emphasis.** The Pathways strategy existed but was less distinctly framed. Investments in dual enrollment, ECHS, and CTE were present but not organized around a quality pathways framework.

**2019-2021: Quality pathways framework development.** The explicit "quality pathways" framing emerged as a way to distinguish high-quality connective programs from low-quality ones that don't improve outcomes. This created more selective grantmaking criteria.

**2021-2023: Workforce integration.** Growing emphasis on the workforce end of pathways — especially middle-skill jobs and sectors accessible without four-year degrees. The strategy increasingly integrated with workforce development investments.

**2024-2025: AI and labor market disruption.** The rapid emergence of AI-driven labor market disruption is creating strategic uncertainty about which pathways will remain valuable. The strategy is beginning to grapple with how AI changes the skills and credentials that lead to good jobs.

## 3.10 Tensions & Tradeoffs

- **Academic rigor vs. workforce alignment:** CTE pathways that are heavily workforce-aligned risk losing academic rigor and narrowing students' options; purely academic pathways may not prepare students for available jobs.
- **Universal vs. targeted design:** Dual enrollment and ECHS programs designed to be selective (for advanced students) vs. open to all (equity-oriented). High-quality programs often become selective; equity requires keeping them accessible.
- **Credential value uncertainty:** Labor market value of credentials shifts with technology and economic change. Pathways built around specific credentials may become misaligned over time.
- **System change vs. individual support:** Navigation and advising help individuals navigate a broken system; the deeper question is whether the system itself is being changed.

# 4. WASHINGTON STATE INITIATIVE (WSI) & CHARTER SCHOOLS

## 4.1 Strategic Intent

Washington State Initiative (WSI) is the Gates Foundation's primary place-based strategy — a concentrated effort to improve postsecondary outcomes in Washington State, where the foundation is headquartered. The strategic intent is to demonstrate that a coordinated, multi-lever approach at the state level can dramatically increase postsecondary attainment and close equity gaps — with Washington State serving as both a beneficiary and a proof-of-concept for broader replication.

The charter school strategy (primarily in Washington State, though national in some aspects) aimed to create high-performing charter schools that demonstrate what's possible for low-income students, particularly in the Washington State context. The 2019 strategic review revealed severe financial sustainability problems with Washington's charter sector and prompted a significant strategy reassessment.

## 4.2 Theory of Change

**WSI Theory of Change:** If the following four levers operate effectively in Washington State, then postsecondary attainment will increase for students from low-income backgrounds and students of color:

1. **Regional Partnerships:** Regional backbone organizations coordinate K-12 schools, community colleges, four-year universities, employers, and community organizations around shared PS attainment goals and data.
2. **Effective Advising:** School counselors and advisors are better trained, better resourced, and supported by data systems to provide effective college and career guidance to all students (not just those with engaged families).
3. **Dual Credit / Dual Enrollment:** More students — especially low-income students — access college-level coursework in high school, accelerating their PS timeline and reducing cost.
4. **FAFSA Completion:** More students complete the FAFSA (Free Application for Federal Student Aid), unlocking financial aid they are entitled to but often don't access due to complexity or lack of support.

**WSI Theory of Scale:** Two complementary pathways to scale:
- **Pathway A (Regional Scaling):** Scaling within Washington State through regional mechanisms — spreading successful approaches through regional partnership organizations to more districts and communities.
- **Pathway B (Statewide Policy Reform):** Statewide policy reforms (advising ratios, dual enrollment access, FAFSA requirements) that institutionalize the approach across the state without requiring foundation grants to each district.

**Charter School Theory of Change:** If high-performing charter schools — with strong instructional models, effective leadership, and a culture of high expectations — operate in underserved communities, then they will demonstrate what's possible for low-income students and create competitive pressure on traditional public schools to improve. The presence of multiple successful charter schools in a region creates a "rising tide" effect.

## 4.3 Core Concepts & Frameworks

**Place-Based Strategy:** A theory of philanthropic influence that concentrates investment in a single geographic area to demonstrate coordinated change, rather than spreading investment thinly across many locations. The WSI is the Gates Foundation's primary example of a place-based strategy in USP.

**Regional Backbone Organizations:** Intermediary organizations that coordinate multiple players (schools, colleges, employers, nonprofits) toward shared postsecondary goals. In WSI, backbone organizations play a convening and data-sharing role. They are analogous to "collective impact" backbone organizations.

**Advising-to-Student Ratios:** The ratio of school counselors to students; a structural determinant of advising quality. The American School Counselor Association recommends 1:250; many schools serving low-income students have ratios of 1:500 or higher. WSI advocates for lower ratios and better advisor training.

**Dual Credit / Running Start:** Washington State's Running Start program allows high school juniors and seniors to take community college courses tuition-free. Dual credit expansion through Running Start is a central WSI lever.

**FAFSA Completion as a KPI:** FAFSA completion by senior students is a measurable, near-term indicator of likely PS enrollment. WSI uses FAFSA completion rates (disaggregated by school, district, and demographics) as a key performance indicator.

**Charter School Sustainability:** Financial viability of charter schools. Washington State charter schools face a structural funding gap: they receive less per-pupil funding than traditional public schools (TPS) because they cannot access local levy dollars (a funding mechanism tied to district boundaries). The estimated gap is approximately $1,700 per pupil. This creates a structural disadvantage that makes financial sustainability extremely difficult, especially at smaller enrollment sizes.

**500-Student Sustainability Threshold:** The Gates Foundation's analysis of Washington charter schools found that approximately 500 students is the enrollment threshold below which a charter school faces insurmountable financial sustainability challenges. Most Washington charter schools are well below this threshold.

**CMO (Charter Management Organization):** A nonprofit organization that operates multiple charter schools. National CMOs (e.g., KIPP, Success Academy) are experienced operators with proven models. One of the strategic errors identified in the 2019 WSI charter review was recruiting California-based CMOs to expand into Washington State; these CMOs faced unfamiliar regulatory environments and funding structures.

## 4.4 Sub-strategies

**Regional Partnership Building:** Funding and supporting backbone organizations (such as Puget Sound Skills Center, regional education service districts) that convene and coordinate across sectors.

**Advising Improvement:** Training school counselors; funding advising tool development; advocating for improved counselor-to-student ratios.

**Dual Credit Expansion:** Supporting Running Start participation, especially for low-income students who face access barriers (transportation, course availability, information).

**FAFSA Completion Campaigns:** Funding FAFSA completion support programs, near-peer outreach, and school-level technical assistance.

**Charter School Support and Assessment:** Providing financial and operational support to existing Washington State charter schools while assessing long-term viability. The 2019 review prompted a more honest assessment of which schools are viable.

**Charter School Wind-Down Planning:** Recognizing that many Washington State charter schools are not financially viable, developing responsible wind-down plans that protect students.

## 4.5 Key Vocabulary

- **WSI (Washington State Initiative):** Gates Foundation's place-based PS strategy in Washington State
- **Regional backbone:** Coordinating organization in WSI theory of change
- **Running Start:** WA state dual enrollment program for 11th and 12th graders
- **FAFSA (Free Application for Federal Student Aid):** Federal financial aid application; completion is a WSI KPI
- **Theory of Scale (ToS):** How WSI impact spreads beyond direct grantees (two pathways: regional and policy)
- **Charter school sustainability:** Financial viability of charter schools; structural funding gap ~$1,700/pupil vs TPS
- **500-student threshold:** Minimum enrollment for financial sustainability of WA charter schools
- **CMO (Charter Management Organization):** Multi-school charter operator
- **TPS (Traditional Public Schools):** Non-charter public schools; the baseline comparison for charter outcomes
- **CREDO:** Stanford-based charter school research center; produced key WA charter school outcome data
- **Green Dot:** A California-based CMO that expanded to Washington; faced declining enrollment challenges
- **Enrollment cliff:** Risk of rapid enrollment decline when a charter school underperforms or loses community trust
- **Wind-down costs:** The costs of responsibly closing a charter school ($35.3M estimated for WA sector)
- **ELL (English Language Learners):** Students still developing English proficiency; WA charter CREDO data showed ELL students outperforming TPS peers

## 4.6 Named Programs, Initiatives, and Grantees

- **Puget Sound Educational Service District / Regional backbone organizations:** WSI coordination partners
- **College Spark Washington:** Foundation-linked entity supporting college access in WA
- **Washington Student Achievement Council (WSAC):** State agency for postsecondary attainment; policy partner
- **Green Dot Public Schools:** California-based CMO that expanded to Washington State; one of the larger charter operators
- **Summit Public Schools:** KIPP-aligned charter network with strong reading outcomes in CREDO data
- **CREDO (Center for Research on Education Outcomes, Stanford):** Research partner; produced charter school outcome studies including WA-specific analysis
- **Washington State Charter Schools Association:** Sector association for WA charter schools
- **State Board of Education (WA):** Authorizer of charter schools in Washington

## 4.7 Outcome Metrics

**WSI Metrics:**
- FAFSA completion rates (by district, school, demographics)
- Dual enrollment participation rates (Running Start)
- Postsecondary enrollment rates (immediately after HS graduation)
- Postsecondary completion rates (1, 2, 4, 6-year)
- Advising quality measures (counselor-to-student ratios, advising session frequency and quality)

**Charter School Metrics:**
- Academic outcomes vs. TPS (CREDO virtual control record methodology)
- Enrollment trends (stability, growth, or decline)
- Financial sustainability indicators (per-pupil funding vs. cost structure)
- ELL and special education student outcomes
- Graduation rates

**CREDO Findings (Washington State, 2019):**
- ELL students in WA charter schools significantly outperformed TPS peers
- Black and Hispanic student gains were positive but statistically insignificant
- Summit high schools showed strong reading outcomes
- Green Dot schools performed below TPS on average but showed improvement trends

## 4.8 Geographic & Demographic Focus

**Geographic focus:** Washington State, with particular attention to the Puget Sound region (Seattle, Tacoma, Bellevue) and Eastern Washington (Yakima, Spokane), where poverty concentrations and demographic diversity are highest.

**Demographic focus:** Low-income students, students of color (especially Hispanic/Latino and Black students, who represent a disproportionate share of WA charter school students), and first-generation college students. ELL students are a particularly significant subgroup given WA's agricultural economy and immigrant workforce.

## 4.9 Strategic Shifts & Inflection Points

**2012-2016: Charter authorization and launch.** Washington State passed charter school legislation in 2012 (after multiple failed ballot initiatives). Early charter schools opened, many with significant Gates Foundation support. National CMOs were recruited to expand into Washington, bringing established models.

**2016-2018: Judicial and financial challenges.** The Washington Supreme Court ruled the charter school law unconstitutional (2015); the legislature passed a revised law (2016). Schools survived but faced uncertainty. Financial sustainability problems became apparent.

**2019: Strategic review.** A comprehensive internal review of Washington State charter school results found severe financial sustainability problems: only 1 of 12 schools reviewed was financially sustainable; the estimated sector wind-down cost was $35.3M; the California CMO recruitment strategy was identified as a strategic error. CREDO results showed mixed academic outcomes.

**2019-2025: Gradual reassessment.** The charter strategy has been significantly scaled back. Continued support is more targeted to schools with viable paths to sustainability. WSI's four levers (regional partnerships, advising, dual credit, FAFSA) became the primary strategic focus.

**2025: WSI as a mature place-based model.** WSI has become a relatively mature strategy with established backbone organizations and documented approaches. The theory of scale (Pathway B — statewide policy reform) has become the primary long-term scaling mechanism.

## 4.10 Tensions & Tradeoffs

- **Charter school mission vs. financial reality:** The foundation is committed to the students in charter schools and the communities they serve, but the financial analysis shows many schools are not viable. Protecting students requires honest acknowledgment of financial constraints.
- **National CMO models vs. local context:** National CMOs bring proven models and operational expertise but may not fit the specific regulatory, cultural, and financial context of Washington State. The CMO recruitment strategy from California was a documented strategic error.
- **Place-based depth vs. national breadth:** Concentrating investment in Washington State creates depth and coherence but limits national reach. The theory of scale (Pathway B) is the answer — policy changes in WA spread the model without requiring direct foundation grants to every district.
- **Advising supply vs. structural barriers:** Better advising can help individual students navigate barriers to college access, but structural barriers (cost, credit transfer gaps, FAFSA complexity) limit what better advising alone can accomplish.

# 5. USP DATA STRATEGY

## 5.1 Strategic Intent

The USP Data strategy aims to build the data infrastructure, evidence base, and analytical capacity necessary to drive improvement across all USP strategy areas. Data is not an end in itself; the strategic intent is to ensure that data systems, tools, and evidence serve the specific improvement priorities of the Gates Foundation's U.S. Program — accelerating learning, improving student outcomes, and advancing equity.

The 2025 iteration represents a major AI pivot: the USP Data strategy is repositioning from "data infrastructure" to "AI-era education intelligence" — building the technical foundations that allow AI systems to provide personalized, contextually appropriate support to students, teachers, and institutions.

## 5.2 Theory of Change

**2020 Theory of Change (DSGU Framework):** If reliable, interoperable education data systems (Data Systems for Getting to Understanding — DSGU) are built and sustained, providing actionable intelligence to educators and institutions, then educators will make better instructional decisions, institutions will improve support systems, and students will achieve better outcomes.

**2025 Theory of Change (AI Pivot):** If AI systems have access to reliable, semantically consistent, portable student data (Portable Semantic Memory), then AI tutoring, advising, and support tools will be able to provide personalized, contextually appropriate support to each student — accelerating learning and improving equity. The data layer is the foundation on which AI education tools depend.

The 2025 theory adds a second strand: **If** trusted, rigorous evidence is built and communicated about what works in education (especially AI-assisted education), **then** practitioners, policymakers, and funders will make better decisions about which tools and approaches to adopt and scale.

## 5.3 Core Concepts & Frameworks

**DSGU (Data Systems for Getting to Understanding):** The organizing framework for the 2020 USP Data strategy. DSGU emphasizes building longitudinal, interoperable student data systems that connect K-12, postsecondary, and workforce data. Texas was the focal state for DSGU implementation, with $60M committed over three years.

**Portable Semantic Memory:** The core technical concept of the 2025 AI pivot. A "portable semantic memory" is a representation of a student's learning history, preferences, context, and goals that is:
- **Portable:** Transferable across systems, schools, and platforms (not locked in a single vendor's system)
- **Semantic:** Meaningfully structured so AI systems can interpret and use the data (not just raw records)
- **Persistent:** Maintained over time as the student progresses

Portable Semantic Memory is the education analog of a patient's electronic health record — a structured, portable data representation that enables any qualified provider to understand and serve the patient (or student).

**MCP (Model Context Protocol):** A technical standard that enables AI systems to share structured context. The USP Data team sees MCP as the infrastructure layer for portable semantic memory — similar to how FHIR standards enabled electronic health records interoperability in healthcare.

**FHIR (Fast Healthcare Interoperability Resources):** The healthcare data standard that enabled electronic health records to be portable and interoperable across health systems. The USP Data strategy explicitly uses FHIR as an analogy and aspiration for education data standards.

**Three Pillars of the 2025 USP Data Strategy:**
1. **Enable AI Solutions:** Build the data infrastructure that allows AI education tools to function effectively — especially portable semantic memory and aligned semantic standards.
2. **Build Trusted Evidence:** Fund rigorous research and evaluation that builds an evidence base for what works in AI-assisted education.
3. **Accelerate Feedback Loops:** Create faster, more actionable feedback mechanisms connecting data to practitioners and decision-makers.

**Interoperability:** The ability of different data systems to share and use each other's data. Interoperability is a persistent technical and political challenge in education — vendors have incentives to lock in customers, and systems rarely talk to each other.

**Education-to-Workforce (E-W) Indicators Framework:** A research investment ($4.2M, 2021-2024) developing 20 critical questions for understanding how education connects to workforce outcomes. Applied in 12 states; used to guide data system investments.

**Postsecondary Data Partnership (PDP):** See PS Strategy section; also a USP Data investment. NSC-managed platform covering ~1/3 of PS enrollment.

**Infinite Campus:** A major K-12 student information system (SIS) vendor. The foundation had an R&D partnership with Infinite Campus focused on data system development and interoperability.

**BrightHive:** A data governance and data sharing platform company. Matt Gee, director of USP Data, is the founder of BrightHive. This creates a notable conflict-of-interest question that is a potential tension within the strategy.

## 5.4 Sub-strategies

**Data Infrastructure Investment (DSGU/Texas focus, 2020):** Building longitudinal student data systems in focal states, with Texas as the primary investment. Focus on K-12 to PS to workforce data linkage.

**Vendor Partnerships (Infinite Campus):** Working with major SIS vendors to improve data system capabilities, interoperability, and research value.

**Portable Semantic Memory Development (2025):** Investing in technical standards and platform development to enable portable, AI-readable student data representations.

**Evidence Building:** Funding rigorous evaluations of AI tutoring tools, data-driven practices, and education technology efficacy. Building on IES standards.

**Feedback Loop Acceleration:** Developing dashboards, early warning systems, and practitioner-facing data tools that put actionable data in educators' hands in real time.

**MCP/Standard Setting:** Investing in technical standard-setting efforts (analogous to FHIR in healthcare) that would enable semantic interoperability across education AI systems.

## 5.5 Key Vocabulary

- **DSGU:** Data Systems for Getting to Understanding; 2020 framework
- **Portable Semantic Memory:** Structured, portable student data representation enabling AI personalization
- **MCP (Model Context Protocol):** Technical standard for AI context sharing; analogous to FHIR
- **FHIR:** Healthcare interoperability standard; aspirational analog for education
- **Interoperability:** Ability of data systems to share and use each other's data
- **SIS (Student Information System):** School database tracking student attendance, grades, enrollment; e.g., Infinite Campus, PowerSchool
- **E-W Indicators Framework:** Education-to-Workforce indicators; 20 critical questions; $4.2M investment
- **PDP (Postsecondary Data Partnership):** NSC-managed PS data platform
- **Longitudinal data:** Student data tracked over time, across multiple years and institutions
- **Data linkage:** Connecting records across different data systems (K-12, PS, workforce)
- **Matt Gee:** Director, USP Data; founder of BrightHive (a data governance company)
- **BrightHive:** Data governance/sharing platform company; founded by USP Data director
- **Incremental budget request:** The 2025 AI pivot strategy included an incremental budget request of $142M-$174M above baseline
- **Bill Gates use case request:** In reviewing the 2025 strategy, Bill Gates specifically asked the team to sharpen and specify the AI use cases before proceeding
- **Common Core continuity question:** A question raised in 2025 strategy review about whether CCSS (Common Core) alignment standards are still required for data systems and AI tools, given the political context

## 5.6 Named Programs, Initiatives, and Grantees

- **National Student Clearinghouse (NSC):** PDP operator; also provides enrollment and completion tracking data
- **Infinite Campus:** SIS vendor; R&D partnership on data system development

## 5.7 Outcome Metrics

- **Data system coverage:** Share of students in states/districts with DSGU-aligned data systems
- **Interoperability metrics:** Percentage of education data systems able to exchange data with PDP or other shared platforms
- **PDP coverage:** Share of national PS enrollment covered by PDP (reached ~1/3 by 2022)
- **Evidence base quality:** Number of rigorous studies on AI tutoring and data-driven practices; effect sizes
- **Practitioner usage:** Share of educators and institutions actively using data dashboards and early warning systems
- **Portable Semantic Memory adoption:** (Emerging metric) Number of platforms and institutions implementing portable semantic memory standards
- **Budget milestones:** Completion of the $142M-$174M incremental investment request with clear use case definitions

## 5.8 Geographic & Demographic Focus

**Geographic focus:** Initially concentrated in focal states (Texas for DSGU) but increasingly national through PDP and standard-setting. Texas is notable for its large, diverse student population and its relatively strong longitudinal data infrastructure.

**Demographic focus:** The data strategy is a means, not an end — its demographic focus is defined by the end-goal strategies it serves. Equity considerations include ensuring AI systems trained on historical data don't perpetuate historical inequities, and that portable semantic memory systems protect student privacy especially for marginalized groups.

## 5.9 Strategic Shifts & Inflection Points

**2017-2019: Data systems focus.** USP Data investments were primarily focused on building data infrastructure — longitudinal student data systems, data linkage, and interoperability. The primary use case was institutional research and evaluation.

**2020: DSGU framework launch.** The DSGU framework organized data investments around three goals: understanding student learning, understanding what works, and enabling continuous improvement. Texas became the focal state with a $60M commitment.

**2021-2023: Vendor partnerships and PDP expansion.** Infinite Campus R&D partnership deepened; PDP expanded. The E-W Indicators Framework was developed. Growing attention to the PS-workforce data linkage problem.

**2024-2025: AI pivot.** The emergence of LLMs and AI tutoring tools fundamentally reframed the USP Data strategy. The team recognized that AI education tools are only as good as the data they can access — and that the education data ecosystem is poorly prepared for the AI era. The Portable Semantic Memory concept emerged as the north star for a new strategy.

**2025: Budget request and leadership scrutiny.** The $142M-$174M incremental investment request prompted close scrutiny from leadership, including Bill Gates personally asking the team to sharpen its use cases. The Common Core continuity question was also raised — whether CCSS alignment is still required given political dynamics.

## 5.10 Tensions & Tradeoffs

- **Privacy vs. utility:** Richer, more portable student data creates more powerful AI personalization — but also creates greater privacy risks, especially for marginalized students. Student data privacy is a federal (FERPA) and state law issue.
- **Vendor lock-in vs. open standards:** Vendors (Infinite Campus, PowerSchool, Canvas, etc.) have commercial incentives to make their data systems proprietary. The foundation's investment in open standards and interoperability is in tension with vendor commercial interests.
- **Matt Gee / BrightHive conflict:** The director of USP Data founded a company (BrightHive) that competes in the data governance space. This is a potential conflict of interest that creates reputational risk.
- **Speed vs. rigor:** AI tools are developing rapidly. The evidence base for their efficacy is thin. Waiting for rigorous evidence risks falling behind the technology curve; proceeding without evidence risks funding ineffective or harmful tools.
- **Common Core politics:** If portable semantic memory standards require CCSS alignment, the strategy inherits the political toxicity of Common Core in some states. The 2025 review raised this as an open question.
- **2045 winddown:** With the foundation planning to cease operations by ~2045, the USP Data strategy must either embed standards and infrastructure in durable public institutions (like NSC, state agencies) or acknowledge that investments will not be sustained.

# 6. USPAC (U.S. POLICY ADVOCACY & COMMUNICATIONS)

## 6.1 Strategic Intent

USPAC (U.S. Policy Advocacy & Communications) is the Gates Foundation's vehicle for influencing federal and state education policy — both advocating for specific policy changes and building the political and communications environment that makes reform possible. The strategic intent is to shape the policy conditions that enable the other USP strategy areas to succeed: if K-12 and PS strategies require favorable policy environments (curriculum transparency, financial aid reform, data policy), USPAC creates those environments.

USPAC also operates defensively — monitoring and responding to policy changes that could harm foundation investments or set back progress on equity and educational quality.

## 6.2 Theory of Change

**If** the foundation builds a strong, credible, bipartisan network of policy champions, funds rigorous policy research and effective communications, and positions itself as a trusted voice in education policy debates, **then** it will be able to advance specific policy reforms (financial aid simplification, performance-based funding, data transparency, instructional quality standards) and build an environment where equity-focused education reform has sustained political support.

The USPAC theory of change has a distinctive political realism: it acknowledges that education policy is deeply contested, that political coalitions shift, and that the foundation must operate in both offensive (advancing reforms) and defensive (blocking harmful changes) modes.

## 6.3 Core Concepts & Frameworks

**Four Policy Pillars (2019 framework):** USPAC organized its work around four areas:
1. **Data:** Education data policy — transparency, privacy, interoperability, longitudinal data system funding
2. **Finance:** Education finance policy — equitable funding, performance-based funding, financial aid reform
3. **Pathways:** Policy enabling high-quality pathways — dual enrollment, CTE, credit transfer, FAFSA
4. **Instructional Supports:** Policies supporting HQIM adoption, professional development standards, curriculum transparency

**Champion Network:** The strategy relies on building and maintaining a network of policy champions — elected officials, state education commissioners, advocates, and thought leaders — who can advance foundation-aligned policy positions. The 2019 review identified a significant champion attrition problem: turnover in elected offices had reduced the network's strength. A specific data point: 79 of 100 key senators who had supported PEPFAR (a proxy for international development champions the foundation works with) were no longer in office by 2019.

**Defensive Posture (Trump-era strategy):** During the first Trump administration (2017-2021), USPAC adopted a primarily defensive posture — protecting existing programs (Title I, IDEA, Pell Grants) from proposed cuts, and resisting rollbacks of equity-focused regulations. The 2019 strategy review codified this defensive orientation.

**Targeted Universalism:** A policy design principle articulated by john powell at UC Berkeley's Othering & Belonging Institute. The principle: set universal goals, but design targeted strategies to reach those goals for different groups. USPAC has used targeted universalism as a frame for resisting the false choice between universal and targeted programs.

**Two-Repayment-Plan Framework:** A USPAC-supported federal student loan reform proposal structuring federal loans into two plans: income-driven repayment (IDR) for borrowers with incomes below a threshold, and standard repayment for higher earners. This is positioned as a more equitable alternative to blanket debt forgiveness.

**First-Dollar Free / Last-Dollar Free:** Two approaches to free college programs. First-dollar free covers all costs before other aid is applied (more beneficial for low-income students who receive Pell and other aid). Last-dollar free covers only costs remaining after other aid is applied (benefits middle-class students who don't receive Pell as much). USPAC supports first-dollar free.

**Minimum Title IV Accountability Standards:** USPAC supports establishing minimum institutional performance standards (graduation rates, earnings outcomes, debt-to-earnings ratios) that institutions must meet to remain eligible for federal financial aid (Title IV eligibility). This is a market accountability mechanism for PS quality.

## 6.4 Sub-strategies

**Federal Policy Advocacy:** Direct engagement with Congress and the U.S. Department of Education on key legislative and regulatory priorities — FAFSA simplification, Pell Grant expansion, Title IV accountability, HEA reauthorization, ESEA/ESSA implementation.

**State Policy Advocacy:** Supporting state-level advocates and policymakers on education finance reform, curriculum transparency, dual enrollment policy, and data system investment.

**Communications and Narrative:** Investing in communications infrastructure — research communication, earned media, coalition messaging — to shift public and policymaker understanding of education quality and equity.

**Champion Development:** Identifying, developing, and sustaining a network of policy champions across party lines. Supporting bipartisan or non-partisan framing of education reforms.

**Policy Research:** Funding independent policy research organizations (CAP, New America, Third Way, CBPP) to produce credible analyses that inform policy debates.

**Coalition Building:** Building formal and informal coalitions around specific policy campaigns — FAFSA simplification, Pell expansion, instructional quality.

## 6.5 Key Vocabulary

- **USPAC:** U.S. Policy Advocacy & Communications; Gates Foundation's federal and state policy advocacy function
- **Four pillars:** Data, Finance, Pathways, Instructional Supports
- **Champion network:** Policy supporters and elected officials who advance foundation-aligned positions
- **Champion attrition:** Loss of champions through electoral turnover or changed positions
- **Defensive posture:** Strategy of protecting existing gains against rollback rather than advancing new reforms
- **Targeted universalism:** john powell framework — universal goals, targeted strategies
- **First-dollar free:** Financial aid model covering all costs before other aid (more equitable)
- **Last-dollar free:** Financial aid model covering costs remaining after other aid (less equitable for low-income students)
- **IDR (Income-Driven Repayment):** Student loan repayment plan tied to borrower's income
- **Two-repayment-plan proposal:** USPAC-supported simplification of student loan repayment
- **Title IV:** Federal law authorizing student financial aid; basis for institutional accountability
- **FAFSA simplification:** Reducing FAFSA complexity to increase completion rates
- **Pell Grant expansion:** Increasing maximum Pell Grant amount; $1,000 increase = 3-5% enrollment gain
- **Minimum accountability standards:** Institutional performance floors for Title IV eligibility
- **HEA (Higher Education Act):** Federal law governing postsecondary education; periodically reauthorized
- **ESEA/ESSA (Every Student Succeeds Act):** Federal K-12 education law; reauthorized in 2015
- **79/100 PEPFAR senators:** Cited statistic illustrating champion attrition — 79% of key champions no longer in office
- **Debt forgiveness opposition:** Foundation policy position opposing blanket student debt forgiveness as regressive

- **New America:** Policy research on financial aid, student debt, and higher education access
- **Third Way:** Policy research on equitable value, institutional accountability, and workforce alignment

## 6.9 Strategic Shifts & Inflection Points

**2017-2019: Defensive turn.** The Trump administration's education policy agenda — school choice expansion, Title I portability, rollback of equity regulations — required USPAC to shift to a primarily defensive posture. The 2019 strategy review formalized this.

**2019-2020: Champion attrition crisis.** The assessment of the champion network revealed significant attrition. The response was a renewed effort to identify and develop new champions, particularly at the state level.

**2020: Biden administration transition planning.** The transition from Trump to Biden (confirmed in November 2020) created an opportunity for offensive policy work. USPAC worked with aligned organizations (CAP, New America) on Biden administration priorities — PS accountability, FAFSA simplification, Pell expansion.

**2021-2022: Biden policy window.** The Biden administration's education policy agenda (including American Families Plan proposals for free community college, Pell expansion, IDR reform) created a favorable window. USPAC was actively engaged in federal PS policy debates.

**2023-2025: Trump II preparation.** The second Trump administration (beginning January 2025) again required a defensive posture shift. USPAC strategy documents in 2025 reflect preparation for a hostile federal policy environment — protecting Title I, IDEA, Pell, and equity-focused regulations.

## 6.10 Tensions & Tradeoffs

- **Bipartisan approach vs. advocacy:** Foundation policy positions (HQIM, accountability, first-dollar free) align more naturally with Democratic policy positions. Maintaining bipartisan relationships while taking clear policy positions creates tension.
- **Debt forgiveness:** The foundation opposes blanket debt forgiveness on equity grounds (it benefits higher earners more). This creates tension with progressive allies and student debt advocacy groups.
- **Federal vs. state leverage:** Federal policy has the broadest reach but is harder to move. State policy is more actionable but more fragmented. Balancing the two is a strategic challenge.
- **Transparency and influence:** The Gates Foundation's visible presence in education policy debates generates criticism — concerns about undue influence of a private foundation on public education policy. This creates pressure to either be less visible or to be more explicitly accountable.
- **Champion cultivation and independence:** Champions need to be genuinely independent advocates, not simply foundation spokespeople. Over-coordination can undermine credibility.

# 7. OFFICE OF THE PRESIDENT (OOP) / BROAD USP PORTFOLIO

## 7.1 Strategic Intent

The OOP and broad USP portfolio encompasses strategy work, learning sessions, and investments that cut across specific strategy areas or that operate at the level of overall U.S. Program direction. This includes the CEO and co-chair engagement with strategy, cross-cutting product quality frameworks, educational equity frameworks, and emerging areas that don't fit cleanly within a single strategy bucket.

The strategic intent is to ensure that the U.S. Program as a whole is coherent, learning, and adapting — that individual strategy areas are reinforcing rather than contradicting each other, and that the foundation is positioned to respond to new evidence, political changes, and emerging opportunities.

## 7.2 Theory of Change

The OOP/broad portfolio doesn't have a single theory of change; rather, it operationalizes the meta-theory that **if** the foundation has rigorous learning processes, clear strategic frameworks, strong partnerships, and leadership that is willing to adapt based on evidence, **then** the individual strategy areas will become more effective over time.

Key premises:
- **Learning and adaptation:** The foundation should continuously learn from its investments and be willing to make significant strategic pivots when evidence warrants.
- **Cross-strategy coherence:** K-12, PS, Pathways, WSI, and Data strategies should reinforce each other; the OOP function ensures coherence across these areas.
- **Leadership engagement:** The co-chairs (Bill and Melinda Gates) and CEO engagement with strategy is a significant asset — their involvement signals foundation commitment and creates access.

## 7.3 Core Concepts & Frameworks

**Product Quality Framework (March 2022):** A framework for evaluating the quality of educational products — both HQIM and digital learning tools. Developed with Student Achievement Partners (Phil Daro, Bill McCallum, Jason Zimba), the Product Quality Framework identifies four key dimensions of product quality. The March 2022 OOP session on this framework was a significant strategy moment.

**Four Key Product Metrics (from 2022 session):**
1. **Fidelity of standards alignment:** Does the product actually align to the standards it claims to align to?
2. **Rigor of learning design:** Is the learning design grounded in evidence about how students learn?
3. **Usability and teacher support:** Can teachers actually use the product effectively without extraordinary expertise?
4. **Equity and accessibility:** Is the product accessible to and effective for students from diverse backgrounds?

**MEP Framework (Motivation, Engagement, Persistence):** A framework for understanding student success, presented at a June 2023 OOP session. The MEP model (with a C for Context: C*MEP) understands student outcomes as a function of:
- **C (Context):** The environmental and structural factors shaping a student's experience
- **M (Motivation):** Specifically defined as Expectancy × Value — students are motivated when they believe they can succeed AND that success is worth the effort
- **E (Engagement):** Three dimensions — behavioral (showing up, completing tasks), cognitive (active thinking), emotional (belonging, connection)
- **P (Persistence):** Sustained engagement over time, through setbacks and obstacles

The June 2023 MEP session included: Lisa Means, Camille Farrington, Thanos (no last name in notes), Katerine Haimovitz, and Jim Meyer.

**Co-Chair Position on AI and Relationships:** The co-chairs have expressed a specific concern about AI tutoring: younger students need relationship-based learning, and AI should not replace human relationships. The co-chair position is that AI should augment, not replace, relationship-based instruction — especially for younger students. This has implications for how the foundation evaluates AI tutoring tools.

**Targeted Universalism (OOP framing):** The OOP has used targeted universalism (john powell, UC Berkeley) as a cross-cutting equity framework — set universal goals, design targeted strategies for different groups. This principle appears in both K-12 and PS strategy documents as a way to navigate the tension between universal programs and targeted equity investments.

**Demographic context (OOP-level framing):** The broad strategic context for all USP work: students of color became the majority of US public school enrollment between 2000-2017 (projected 56% by 2029). In 2017-18, 79% of teachers were white, 77.7% of principals were white. This demographic mismatch in the educator pipeline is a structural equity challenge.

**2045 Winddown:** The Gates Foundation has publicly committed to distributing all assets and ceasing operations by approximately 2045. This creates a strategic planning constraint: USP investments must either be able to sustain themselves without foundation support, be embedded in durable public institutions, or be completed within the foundation's operating timeline.

# 8. CROSS-CUTTING THEMES

## 8.1 Equity as a Through-Line

Every USP strategy area is oriented around equity — specifically, closing outcome gaps between students from low-income backgrounds and students of color on one hand, and higher-income white students on the other. The equity framing has deepened over time, moving from "close achievement gaps" (which can imply deficit framing) to "dismantle structural barriers" and "equitable value."

The demographic context is always present: the U.S. school-age population is majority-minority, and is projected to reach 56% students of color by 2029. The educator workforce is 79% white. This demographic mismatch creates a persistent structural challenge — the people making educational decisions are demographically different from the students they serve.

Targeted universalism (john powell) is the philosophical framework for navigating the universal vs. targeted tension: set universal goals (all students should have access to quality education and earn credentials with economic value), but design targeted strategies for the groups who face specific structural barriers.

## 8.2 Evidence Standards and Learning Culture

The foundation has invested heavily in building an evidence-based culture — funding rigorous evaluations, developing evidence frameworks, and being willing to change strategy when evidence is unfavorable (as with the Intensive Partnerships evaluation). Key evidence standards: IES gold standard (RCT or quasi-experimental with rigorous design); effect sizes; and disaggregation by race and income.

The tension: evidence takes time to accumulate, and the foundation is operating in a fast-moving environment (AI, political change, COVID disruption). The appropriate balance between evidence standards and speed of action is a persistent strategic question.

## 8.3 Systems Change vs. Direct Service

The foundation's theory is that it can have the greatest impact by changing systems — policies, norms, data infrastructure, market conditions — rather than by directly delivering services to students. This "systems change" orientation shapes all strategy areas.

The tension: systems change is slow, hard to measure, and requires sustained commitment. Direct service investments (like tutoring programs or advising services) show more visible, immediate impact, but are hard to scale. The foundation has resolved this tension differently in different strategy areas (WSI includes direct service components; K-12 is almost entirely systems-focused).

## 8.4 The AI Inflection Point (2024-2025)

Across all USP strategy areas, the emergence of large language models and AI tutoring tools has created a moment of strategic reflection. Key questions:
- Will AI tools help or widen equity gaps? (Access, device quality, teacher capacity to implement AI tools are distributed unequally.)
- What is the evidence base for AI tutoring efficacy? (Very thin as of 2025; RCTs are only beginning.)
- How should the data infrastructure evolve to support AI? (Portable Semantic Memory concept.)
- What is the role of human relationships in AI-era education? (Co-chair concern about younger students and AI relationships.)

The AI inflection point is touching every strategy area simultaneously, creating both opportunity and uncertainty.

## 8.5 Political Environment and the Defensive Posture

All USP strategies must operate in a politically contested environment. The first Trump administration (2017-2021) required a defensive posture — protecting existing programs. The Biden administration (2021-2025) created an offensive window. The second Trump administration (beginning January 2025) again requires a defensive posture.

The political environment shapes not just advocacy strategy (USPAC) but also every other strategy area: Common Core politics affect K-12; financial aid politics affect PS; data privacy politics affect USP Data; immigration policy affects the ELL student population.

## 8.6 The 2045 Winddown

The foundation's commitment to sunset by approximately 2045 creates a strategic planning constraint across all areas. Strategies must either:
- Build durable public institutions that will sustain without foundation support (e.g., embedding standards in NSC/PDP, building state longitudinal data systems)
- Demonstrate impact and achieve outcomes within the remaining timeline
- Explicitly plan for responsible wind-down

This is particularly relevant for long-timescale investments like data infrastructure and systemic school reform.

## 8.7 Philanthropy's Role and Influence

The Gates Foundation's scale creates unusual influence — and unusual scrutiny. Philanthropy directing billions into public education raises questions about democratic accountability and appropriate roles. The Common Core backlash was in part a backlash against perceived foundation overreach. USP strategies must balance effectiveness with legitimacy.

## 8.8 Bodies of Work (BoW), Theories, and Planning Vocabulary

Across USP, a consistent planning vocabulary structures strategy documents:
- **BoW (Body of Work):** A defined cluster of investments within a strategy area
- **ToC (Theory of Change):** The causal logic connecting investments to outcomes
- **ToA (Theory of Action):** How the foundation will act to achieve the theory of change
- **ToS (Theory of Scale):** How impact will spread beyond direct grantees
- **Theory of Influence:** How the foundation will influence others (policy, market, norm change) to amplify its own investments

## 8.9 Cross-Strategy Data and Shared Infrastructure

The PDP (Postsecondary Data Partnership) and USP Data investments serve multiple strategy areas. Data is not a strategy unto itself — it is infrastructure for K-12, PS, Pathways, and WSI strategies. The cross-cutting nature of data means that USP Data decisions (interoperability standards, privacy frameworks, AI data architecture) have implications for every other USP strategy.

## Ambition 2045 (Amb'45)

### 10.1 What Ambition 2045 Is

"Ambition 2045" — informally "Amb'45" — is the US Program's 20-year strategy and implementation effort that aligns and operates the division through the Gates Foundation's planned closure in 2045. It is not a repackaging of prior work; it is a division-wide strategic reset that sets bold, measurable goals, defines a unified theory of action, and reorganizes leadership accountabilities to deliver on a single shared goal.

**The 2045 Vision (verbatim draft language, November 2025):**
> "By 2045, education will be a true engine of upward mobility. Our systems will ensure that race, ethnicity, gender, and socioeconomic status no longer predict attainment, access, or prosperity. They will accelerate learning across the entire learner journey through personalized navigation, flexible pathways, and technological innovation, while institutions safeguard equity and strengthen learner agency by equipping individuals with the information and support to make informed choices. As a result, learners will earn recognized, high-value credentials that reflect the competencies needed to thrive in their communities and careers."

**The 2045 Goal (finalized March 2026):**
> Double the growth rate of credential attainment among 18–34 year-olds while reducing gaps for historically underserved populations, resulting in 10 million more learners attaining credentials that reflect their skills and knowledge and deliver sustained economic value.

The numeric target behind "double the growth rate" was modeled but left partially in draft form ([x]%) through late 2025; by the March 2026 All Staff it was stated as the "2X rate / 10M more learners" formulation above.

USP sits within the foundation's third overarching 2045 goal — "Hundreds of millions of people break free from poverty" — and is the division that addresses this goal through the U.S. education system as the primary lever.

---

### 10.2 Why Now — Five Converging Factors

Amb'45 was catalyzed by the intersection of five distinct factors, each of which was cited explicitly in source documents:

1. **Foundation Close (2045 Deadline).** In May 2025, Bill Gates publicly announced the Gates Foundation's plan to invest $200 billion over 20 years and close by approximately 2045. This gave USP a clear, finite 20-year horizon — what Allan Golston called "every dollar, every partnership, every bet we make needs to count."

2. **AI Inflection Point.** Advances in artificial intelligence — particularly large language models and adaptive instructional platforms — created a "once-in-a-generation" opportunity to deliver personalized learning and navigation support at scale and at dramatically lower per-student cost. The January 2026 memo described this as: "Advances in AI — when paired with learners and educators — create a new horizon for what is possible." AI also creates risk: exacerbating equity gaps if solutions are not designed with underserved learners at the center.

3. **Internal Fragmentation.** Staff input surfaced "friction, unnecessary complexity, and the need for clearer collaboration and accountability." USP was not organized to achieve a shared, division-wide goal. Allan Golston's January 2026 memo explicitly acknowledged this and announced that reorganization was necessary: "Reorganization of our division is needed to achieve our Ambition 2045 goals."

4. **Budget Finitude.** The foundation's steady-state budget is fixed. The memo described it as using "our generous but finite resources." This required explicit trade-offs and strategic focus rather than maintaining parallel, siloed strategies.

5. **External Acceleration.** The pace of change in AI and education was "accelerating," and USP's work increasingly intersected with the same partners and technologies across previously separate teams (K–12, PS, Pathways, Data). A division-wide strategy was required to leverage shared assets — including relationships with AI hyperscalers like Google and OpenAI — that none of the individual teams could engage as effectively alone.

### 10.3 The Seven 2030 Priorities

Amb'45 organizes USP around seven 2030 priorities. The first four are AI-enabled solution priorities; the final three are cross-cutting enablers. Priorities were named and targets were defined in fall 2025 by delegate groups of USP subject matter experts.

#### Priority 1: Full Stack Core Instruction & Tutoring
**What it is:** AI-enabled high-quality instructional materials (HQIM) integrated with tutoring and adaptive assessment, creating a personalized, feedback-driven learning loop for students below grade level.

**Key momentum point served:** Success in 9th Grade Algebra (passing Algebra I by grade 9).

**2030 Targets:**
- Students below grade level gain **1.6 grade levels in one year** across 25–40 demonstration sites serving 14,000–30,000 students
- Baseline for comparison: below-grade-level students currently gain 0.9 grade levels/year; top 5% of schools reach ~1.3
- Scale product: reach **50–67% of elementary students** (5.5M–7.4M) and **56–74% of middle school students** (3.3M–4.4M) in CA, FL, NY, and TX
- Per-student cost declines to **$60–$80** as platforms scale

**Accountable team:** K-12 Curriculum & Tutoring (formerly led by Bob Hughes; successor recruitment underway as of January 2026)

**Key instruments:** HQIM, tutoring platforms, Modern Math, WSI Adoption Courses, courseware

#### Priority 2: AI-Enabled Gateway Math Courses
**What it is:** AI-enabled gateway math courseware (referred to in documents as "Learnvia") that transforms course design, elevates instruction, and personalizes student support for introductory or prerequisite college math.

**Key momentum point served:** Completion of gateway postsecondary math courses.

**2030 Targets:**
- **Average course pass rates of ~80%** (approximately +10 percentage points over baseline)
- Baseline: universal baseline success rates 70–77%; focus institution baseline 67–72%
- **Equity gaps decrease by ~50%** (current gap is 13–19 pp)
- **355,000 students across 450 institutions** impacted
- **38,000–52,000 additional course completions**
- One-time course development cost: $2–3M per course; **$0 cost to students** via open access distribution

**Accountable team:** PS Courseware & Navigation (Deputy Director: Frank Vahid)

#### Priority 3: AI-Enabled Personalized Advising
**What it is:** AI-enabled personalized advising solutions — spanning the Pathways + WSI platforms and the Digital Holistic Student Supports (DHSS) platform — integrating advising technology, data systems, and human-led models to improve FAFSA completion, enrollment, credit accumulation, and persistence.

**Key momentum point served:** Transition from high school to postsecondary (immediate enrollment and first-year persistence).

**2030 Targets:**
- **FAFSA completion: +7–10 percentage points**
- **Immediate college enrollment: +2–5 pp** (+5 pp for first-generation students)
- **First-year postsecondary retention: +4–7 pp**
- **Postsecondary completion: +5–7 pp** from institutional baselines
- **Scale — high school:** reach 35,000–8.6 million HS students (24%–59% of 14.5M students); upper bound assumes LLM embed with Google Classroom or OpenAI SDK
- **Scale — postsecondary:** reach 300,000 PS students via DHSS
- Per-student cost: $12–$20 by 2030, maintaining at least 20% affordability advantage over existing advising systems
- Baseline: 62% immediate enrollment; 63% PS completion; traditional advising yields 1–3 pp improvement

**Accountable team:** PS Courseware & Navigation (navigation sub-portfolio) and Pathways

#### Priority 4: AI-Enabled Learning Mobility
**What it is:** AI-enabled credit mapping and data transfer tools (CMDTs) that help institutions evaluate, align, and apply credits more efficiently across programs and systems — ensuring every credit earned counts toward a credential.

**Key momentum point served:** Learning counts on credential pathway (credit applicability and transfer).

**2030 Targets:**
- **0 to 1.2–1.6 million learners** use CMDTs by 2030
- **Credit applicability goal: 37%–45%** (up from 32% baseline — currently only ~32% of credits earned by transfer students apply toward degree requirements at the new institution, averaging 10.1 of 31.3 credits per NCES data)
- Institutional cost per learner declines from ~$16 to below $13 (as low as $8–9 at scale)

**Accountable team:** Learning Mobility sub-portfolio (Cheryl Hyman transitioning to Principal Officer role for Learning Mobility on Patrick Methvin's team)

#### Priority 5: AI & Data Infrastructure
**What it is:** The underlying AI and data infrastructure — including AI infrastructure development, partnerships with hyperscalers, benchmarks, datasets, knowledge graphs, and a Data & AI Enablement Hub — that makes the solution priorities work equitably and at scale.

**2030 Targets:**
- **30–50% decrease in the time it takes** for solution partners to reach efficacy targets
- **40% of learners** reached by USP infrastructure investments

**Accountable team:** AI & Data Infrastructure (Nicole Ifill, Deputy Director; Peter Leonard, Deputy Director; roles to be hired for AI & Data Enablement Hub and AI Infrastructure)

#### Priority 6: Exemplars, Public Goods, State Policy, Funding, Communications & Engagement
**What it is:** The "scaling engine" of the division — combining policy, communications, delivery support, and measurement into a coordinated approach that enables the field to adopt, implement, and sustain what works. Jamie McKee's April 2026 delegate group memo articulated this pillar's role as "establishing scale as a discipline for the division."

The pillar's operating model (per the Pillar 6 delegate group):
- **Identify** exemplars and bright spots
- **Measure and learn** what drives those outcomes
- **Strategize for scale** by building conditions that make adoption feasible (policy, public goods, TA, communications)
- **Refine and scale** through differentiated support across contexts

### 10.4 E-W Momentum Points — The 2030 Ladder of Outcomes

Amb'45 is anchored on five prioritized learner-journey milestones, called **E-W (Education-to-Workforce) Momentum Points**, where AI-enabled solutions have the greatest demonstrated likelihood of improving credential attainment. The five momentum points, in sequence:

1. **Passed Algebra I by 9th grade** — foundational math milestone; failure here often forecloses postsecondary opportunity
2. **Quality dual enrollment completion** (gateway course completed in high school) — earning college credit while in HS; access to STEM pathways
3. **Immediate postsecondary enrollment** — transitioning from HS to postsecondary; FAFSA completion is a key leading indicator
4. **Applied learning to credential pathway** — first-year credit accumulation, gateway course completion, persistence
5. **Earned a credential of value** — the ultimate attainment milestone; a recognized, high-value credential with measurable economic returns

These momentum points correspond directly to Priorities 1–4 and form the backbone of the Pillar 6 EW indicator stack. The Pillar 6 delegate group recommended augmenting these five with additional system capacity signals (see Section 10.3, Priority 6 above for the full indicator stack).

The underlying logic: "Evidence shows that the more students who complete these milestones, the more students can earn a credential that brings economic mobility to them, their families, and communities." (Ambition 2045 Memo, November 2025)

### 10.5 Theory of Change and Theory of Action

#### Three Foundational Convictions
The January 2026 "Ambition to Action" memo grounded Amb'45 in three explicit foundational convictions:

1. **Every student has the capacity to learn, grow, and succeed at high levels.** When systems fail to deliver on that promise, it is a design challenge, not a learner deficit. Achieving equitable progress requires a universal commitment to high outcomes for all learners, paired with targeted strategies that address distinct barriers some face.

2. **USP is at a once-in-a-generation inflection point to fully leverage what makes it uniquely effective.** Greater focus and synergy on USP's comparative advantages — building learning systems that accelerate progress, navigation systems that sustain momentum, and delivery mechanisms that enable equitable scale — offers a greater chance to find paths for enduring impact.

3. **Advances in AI — when paired with learners and educators — create a new horizon for what is possible.** USP now has a rare opportunity to engage a common set of actors across its work — including AI hyperscalers and platform leaders — with similar levers to influence how technologies, solutions, and supports evolve, unlocking innovation that is more personalized, accessible, affordable, and faster than previously imaginable.

#### Theory of Action (ToA) Structure
The unified cross-USP Theory of Action was described as still in development as of August 2025, with the following structure emerging:

- **Inputs:** External context (political, technological, social) + strategy inputs
- **Execution:** Solutions, delivery, infrastructure (the seven priorities)
- **Outputs:** Measured impact on Amb'45 goals and outcomes

The ToA organizes around four high-leverage moments in the learner's journey (gateway math, personalized advising, transitions, credit mobility) and recognizes that AI-enabled solutions must be accompanied by delivery infrastructure, policy enablers, and equity guardrails to produce durable impact.

#### "Walking Up the Mountain Backwards" Metaphor
A key framing device used in goal-setting: the guiding principles document describes USP as "walking into the future backwards" — a concept drawn from Indigenous Pacific and Māori worldviews. The future is behind us (unseen, unknowable); the past is in front of us (visible, known). This metaphor means: USP moves forward through time guided by evidence and history rather than by trying to predict an uncertain future. In practice, this means setting bold 2030 targets now, then reassessing and recalibrating at the 2030 horizon before committing to 2035 targets.

The September 2025 deck used the phrase "Walking Up the Mountain Backwards" as a slide header to describe how USP backs into its 2030 goals from the 2045 vision — working from aspirational end-state back to near-term, achievable milestones.

### 10.7 Guiding Principles for Goal-Setting

USLT grounded the goal-setting process in six guiding principles. These were formally documented in June 2025 and referenced throughout strategy documents as the persistent test for whether any goal formulation was sound:

1. **Mission-aligned and impact-driven.** Goals must directly support the foundation's 2045 ambition (break the cycle of poverty; expand paths to prosperity) and USP's vision. They must drive meaningful progress — not just signal intent — and inform real-world decisions, resource allocation, and program design.

2. **Aspirational, achievable, and measurable.** Goals must be bold enough to inspire while remaining grounded in capabilities and comparative advantage. They must include clear outcomes, success indicators, and time frames. 2030 milestones will track progress and ensure alignment.

3. **Equity-centered with a Targeted Universalism approach.** Universal ambitions pursued through strategies tailored to communities and places — particularly those most impacted by systemic barriers. Where appropriate, goals should address working in place in ways that contribute to broader national or systemic progress.

4. **Connected to existing priorities and aligned with work in the field.** Goals should build on and amplify existing foundation and field momentum, align with partner priorities, and leverage the influence of current investments, relationships, and capabilities.

5. **Resonant, communicable, and enduring.** Goals must be clear, compelling, and emotionally resonant to a wide range of stakeholders — staff, grantees, partners, and communities. They must be easily communicated, bridge diverse perspectives, and stand the test of time through shifts in leadership, political context, and technological change (e.g., centering "humanized AI for good").

6. **Grounded in evidence and oriented to the future.** Goals informed by the best available research on effective teaching, learning, economic mobility, and human thriving. USP will "walk into the future backward" — anchored in evidence while anticipating emerging needs and innovations such as AI.

The accompanying "process principles" (how USP will operate during goal-setting) include: acting as one division leadership team; drawing on learnings from previous work; tracking impact through interim goals; working in place to improve delivery; relying on directors to gather team input; consulting external partners when clarity is needed; showing care for staff and partners affected by changes; and being clear about what USP is not doing.

### 10.10 Tensions & Open Questions

The source documents surface several explicit tensions and open questions as of April 2026:

1. **Speed vs. completeness.** USLT committed to sharing decisions quickly even when not all decisions are made, meaning some communications will be incomplete and some uncertainty will persist. The January memo acknowledged: "We do not yet know how all individual colleagues will be affected, nor do we have complete clarity on bodies of work."

2. **AI opportunity vs. equity risk.** AI is simultaneously the engine of the Amb'45 strategy and a potential threat: "Powerful & integrated AI-enabled solutions transform learning and navigation, leading to high value credentials" vs. "Worsened gaps: AI breakthroughs may exacerbate equity gaps due to inequitable access, or quality learning opportunities diverge." The strategy explicitly relies on AI being developed with equity guardrails, but the mechanism for ensuring this at scale through partners and hyperscalers is still being developed.

3. **Targeted universalism in practice.** Amb'45 sets universal goals for all learners but pursues them through targeted strategies. The external research (focus groups, November 2025) found that phrases like "valued credentials" and "measurable economic returns" were interpreted inconsistently or seen as reducing education to financial outcomes. Messaging must balance the economic framing with human-centered language.

4. **Reorganization uncertainty.** As of April 2026, the reorganization is underway but not complete. Individual staff roles are not yet determined. This creates tension between the goal of "operating in our new organizational structure by July 2026" and staff uncertainty about their futures.

5. **WSI and Charters strategic ambiguity.** WSI will not be a standalone strategy by 2030, but the path from current WSI investments to embedded integration is not yet fully defined. Charter strategy is under refresh; the wind-down of Angela Jones's leadership creates additional transition management complexity.

6. **Fixed 2030 goals in a rapidly changing AI environment.** The strategy is explicitly built for flexibility: "Although our vision remains the same, USP will need to be ready to pivot when we encounter breakthroughs, shocks, or other disruptions." This tension between committing to 2030 targets and staying adaptive is managed through the horizons framework — re-assessing and recalibrating at each horizon.

7. **Staff skepticism and change management.** Internal pulse surveys and the structure of monthly office hours reflect acknowledged uncertainty and anxiety among staff. USLT noted: "We know this is a challenging time. We feel it, too. And we do not expect anyone to hold this moment alone." Kara Marchione and Gaby Torres are explicitly named as direct support resources.

8. **Caution about AI overreach from external audiences.** The November 2025 focus group research found that external audiences (parents, K–12 educators, higher education leaders) were "uneasy about AI," saw "AI-enabled support" as vague and potentially replacing rather than assisting teachers, and showed "skepticism about feasibility." Trust depends on transparency, credible evidence, and tangible results — not messaging alone.

### 10.12 Key Amb'45 Vocabulary

New and Amb'45-specific terms used across source documents:

**Ambition 2045 / Amb'45:** The 20-year strategy and implementation effort for USP, anchored to the Gates Foundation's planned 2045 closure. Both a strategic direction and a reorganization effort.

**Horizons:** Five-year planning segments within the 20-year Amb'45 arc. Horizon 1 = now to 2030; Horizon 2 = 2030–2035; Horizon 3 = 2035–2040; Horizon 4 = 2040–2045. At the end of each horizon, USLT will "pull up," reassess, and recalibrate goals for the next horizon. This build-in flexibility is deliberate given AI uncertainty.

**E-W Momentum Points:** Education-to-Workforce Momentum Points — the five prioritized milestones in the learner journey where intervention has the highest leverage for credential attainment. See Section 10.4.

**Credential of Value:** A recognized, high-value credential (degree, certificate, or other recognized credential) that reflects competencies and provides measurable economic returns to the learner. The defining end-state of the Amb'45 learner journey.

**Targeted Universalism:** USP's equity framework for Amb'45. Set universal goals for all learners; pursue them through strategies targeted at the barriers facing those most underserved. Different from a purely universal approach (same strategy for all) or a purely targeted approach (only help specific populations). Allows USP to remain mission-aligned to all learners while concentrating resources on those furthest from goals.

**Full Stack Core Instruction & Tutoring:** Priority 1. The integrated combination of HQIM (curricular foundation) + AI tutoring (personalized learning acceleration) + adaptive assessment (feedback loop). "Full stack" signals that all three layers must be present and integrated — not siloed — for the 1.6 grade levels/year acceleration target to be achievable.

**DHSS (Digital Holistic Student Supports):** The PS postsecondary advising and support platform anchoring Priority 3 for the postsecondary advising scale target (300,000 PS students).

**CMDTs (Credit Mapping and Data Transfer Tools):** The AI-enabled tools at the core of Priority 4 (Learning Mobility) that help institutions evaluate, align, and apply credits across programs and systems.

**Learnvia:** Name used in documents for the AI-enabled gateway math courseware platform at the center of Priority 2. Described as transforming course design, elevating instruction, and personalizing student support.

**Hyperscalers:** Large AI platform companies (Google, OpenAI, and others) referenced in USP documents as strategic channel partners for deploying AI-enabled education solutions at scale. Amb'45 specifically calls out the opportunity to engage hyperscalers as a shared lever across multiple priorities simultaneously.

**Breakthrough Innovations (Priority 7):** High-risk, high-reward investments within and adjacent to the four solution areas that could "fundamentally change the stakes" and "generate impact for the next horizon." Designed to prepare the field for Horizon 2 and beyond. Distinct from the incremental innovation embedded within Priorities 1–4.

**EW Indicator Stack:** The division-wide set of education-to-workforce indicators recommended by the Pillar 6 delegate group as a shared measurement foundation. Organized into four tiers: (1) Foundational Academic Signals (Grade 3/8 proficiency, Algebra I by Grade 9); (2) Momentum and Transition Signals (9th grade on track, FAFSA completion, dual enrollment access); (3) Progression and Attainment Signals (PS enrollment, first-year credit accumulation, gateway course completion, PS persistence, economic mobility); (4) System Capacity and Scaling Signals (equitable placement in rigorous coursework, 8th grade Algebra availability, effective school leadership).

**Walking Into the Future Backwards / Walking Up the Mountain Backwards:** USP's guiding metaphor for evidence-anchored long-horizon planning, drawn from Indigenous Pacific and Māori worldviews. The past (what we know) is in front of us; the future (unknown) is behind. We move forward guided by evidence, not prediction — committing to near-term milestones while remaining open to recalibration.

**Delegate Groups:** Internal working groups of USP subject matter experts convened by USLT to define targets and recommendations for specific Amb'45 priorities. Fall 2025 delegate groups set 2030 targets for Priorities 1–4. Early 2026 delegate groups explored strategy and portfolio-level decisions for all seven priorities.

**Pillar 6:** Colloquial name for Priority 6 (Exemplars, Public Goods, State Policy, Funding, Communications & Engagement). Led by Jamie McKee. The "scaling engine" of the division.

**BOW (Body of Work):** USP-internal term for the portfolio of grants, strategies, and investments within a given team's remit. Deputy Director accountabilities memos explicitly note that role descriptions "do not represent BOW or people-related decisions" — meaning the org chart changes do not yet determine which grants or strategies continue.
